# Genomic Prediction Benchmark

> The experiential core of Phase 4. You predict a quantitative trait (e.g. grain yield) from genome-wide markers on a real dataset, put seven model families on one identical cross-validation starting line, report cross-validated predictive ability with honest uncertainty, and then dissect *why* the winner wins — marker-effect analysis and error analysis, not just a leaderboard. This is the **working seed for the Phase 4 capstone** [[project-2-genomic-prediction-benchmark|Genomic Prediction Benchmark (capstone)]] — the harness you build here is what the capstone scales up to a full panel, a richer feature analysis, and a written report.

## Why this project
The headline question in genomic selection is deceptively simple: given genome-wide markers, how well can you predict an unobserved phenotype or breeding value? The honest answer, for most polygenic traits, is that the classical linear mixed model is very hard to beat — and learning *why* that is so, by your own hands and on real data, is the actual education. This benchmark forces you to put GBLUP/RR-BLUP, Ridge, Elastic Net, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a small neural net on the same starting line: identical folds, identical preprocessing, identical evaluation, so the only thing that varies is the model. That is the discipline that separates a defensible benchmark from a leaderboard accident.

It mirrors real computational-breeding work. The decision a breeding program faces is rarely "which model is fanciest" but "which model gives reliable predictive ability per unit of data, compute, and interpretability — and does it generalize to the genotypes I actually have to predict next cycle?" Proving you can take a raw genotype-plus-phenotype dataset to a fair, leakage-aware comparison table, a set of predicted-vs-observed plots, and a written verdict you would defend, is the single most portable thing you produce in Phase 4. It is **strong portfolio material**: the write-up — *why the fancy model didn't win my genomic-prediction benchmark* — is a `post` candidate that says, in one artifact, "I understand genomic prediction as a practitioner, not a Kaggler."

This is the **working seed for a capstone.** The shared cross-validation harness, the preprocessing pipeline, and the six-plus-method comparison you assemble here are exactly the infrastructure the capstone [[project-2-genomic-prediction-benchmark|Genomic Prediction Benchmark (capstone)]] scales up — to a larger crop panel, deeper marker-effect and error analysis, and a full written report tying "which loci associate" (the GWAS capstone) to "how well the whole genome predicts." Treat every script here as capstone infrastructure, not throwaway.

## Concepts it forces you to apply
- [[genomic-prediction-works-because-markers-in-ld-with-causal-variants-let-a-marker-relationship-matrix-estimate-realized-relatedness|Genomic prediction works because markers in LD with causal variants let a marker-based relationship matrix estimate realized relatedness]] — this is *the* mechanism the whole benchmark tests, and it explains both why GBLUP and Ridge are so strong on additive polygenic traits and why predictive ability decays as test individuals become less related to the training set (your error analysis makes that decay visible).
- [[heritability-is-a-property-of-a-population-in-an-environment-not-of-a-trait|Heritability is a property of a population in an environment, not of a trait]] — the trait's heritability *in this panel and environment* is the ceiling on achievable predictive accuracy; you cannot predict variance that is environmental noise, so a "low" correlation may be the trait's ceiling, not your model's failure.
- [[the-breeders-equation-says-response-to-selection-equals-narrow-sense-heritability-times-the-selection-differential|The breeder's equation says response to selection equals narrow-sense heritability times the selection differential]] — context for *why* predictive ability matters at all: a GEBV is only useful insofar as selecting on it produces genetic gain, and accuracy enters the genomic version of this equation directly.
- The four headline claims of [[p4m1-classical-genomic-prediction-gblup-and-rr-blup|p4m1 · Classical Genomic Prediction: GBLUP and RR-BLUP]] — that prediction rides on LD-reconstructed realized relatedness; that the G matrix beats a pedigree; that *RR-BLUP on markers and GBLUP on the G matrix are the same model viewed two ways* (e.g. evergreen slug `rr-blup-on-markers-and-gblup-on-the-g-matrix-are-the-same-model-viewed-two-ways`, to be written in p4m1); and that prediction *must* shrink marker effects because markers vastly outnumber individuals — are all *applied* here, not recited. The RR-BLUP↔GBLUP equivalence in particular keeps you from "double-counting" the linear baseline.
- The contender-ranking claims of [[p4m2-ml-approaches-ridge-elastic-net-rf-gradient-boosting|p4m2 · ML Approaches]] — *for additive traits, penalized linear models usually beat tree ensembles* (e.g. slug `for-additive-traits-penalized-linear-models-usually-beat-tree-ensembles-in-genomic-prediction`); *the honest metric is cross-validated predictive ability, not training fit* (e.g. slug `the-honest-metric-for-genomic-prediction-is-cross-validated-predictive-ability-not-training-fit`); and *ML earns its keep only where non-additive effects or G×E matter* (e.g. slug `ml-earns-its-keep-in-genomic-prediction-only-where-non-additive-effects-or-gxe-matter`) — are the hypotheses this benchmark tests directly. *(These evergreen notes are written as you work p4m1–p4m2; link them in once they exist.)*
- The deep-learning claims of [[p4m3-deep-learning-on-genomic-data|p4m3 · Deep Learning on Genomic Data]] — *on tabular genotype matrices deep nets frequently fail to beat GBLUP* (e.g. slug `on-tabular-genotype-matrices-deep-nets-frequently-fail-to-beat-gblup`) and *deep learning's advantage depends on sample size relative to markers* (e.g. slug `deep-learnings-advantage-in-genomic-prediction-depends-on-large-sample-size-relative-to-markers`) — are exactly what the neural-net row tests on 599 lines. *(Forward links — write the notes in p4m3, then wire them here.)*
- The critical-reading standard of [[p4m4-reading-agricultural-ml-papers|p4m4 · Reading Agricultural ML Papers]] — *a fair cross-validation must hold out the unit the method claims to predict* and *relatedness leakage across folds inflates reported accuracy* — is the bar you hold *your own* benchmark to, not just the papers you read.

## Setup
- **Language / tools:**
  - **Python** is the spine: `numpy` and `pandas` for the genotype matrix; **scikit-learn** for `Ridge`, `ElasticNetCV`, `RandomForestRegressor`, the `KFold`/`GroupKFold` cross-validation harness, and the shared scorer; **XGBoost** for gradient boosting; a **small neural net** (an MLP, optionally a 1-D CNN) in PyTorch or Keras. Confirm package versions and the CV/scorer APIs against current docs when you start (see gaps).
  - **R** for the classical baseline: **rrBLUP** (`mixed.solve`, `A.mat`, `kin.blup`) and/or **BGLR** (`BGLR()` with `BRR`/`BayesB`/`RKHS`) for GBLUP and RR-BLUP. The BGLR `wheat` data objects live here too. Run R either as a separate baseline script whose held-out predictions you import, or via `rpy2` — but the *folds must be identical* across R and Python (share the fold-assignment vector as a file). Verify exact citation details for rrBLUP and BGLR when you write up (see gaps).
  - Pin every version in a lockfile; fix all random seeds; keep raw data read-only.
- **Data:** the **BGLR `wheat` dataset** — 599 historical CIMMYT wheat lines genotyped at **1,279 DArT markers** (coded 0/1), with **grain yield** phenotyped across **four mega-environments** (`wheat.Y` has one column per environment; `wheat.A` is the pedigree relationship matrix; `wheat.X` is the marker matrix). It is a classic, small, fully public genomic-selection benchmark and the same dataset p4m1–p4m3 use, so your modules' work drops straight in. Pick **one** environment's grain yield as the target trait for the headline benchmark (note which one; environments differ in heritability). *Alternative:* a public maize or rice panel for a larger-n contrast — verify a currently-live link before relying on it (see gaps). See [[reading-list|the reading list]] for dataset cards.
- **Environment:** **one** preprocessing pipeline (any imputation, MAF filtering if you re-derive markers, centering/standardization) applied *identically* to every model; a **single shared outer-CV split object** (one fold-assignment vector) reused by every method so the comparison is genuinely apples-to-apples; all hyperparameter tuning done with **inner** cross-validation on the training folds only, never touching the held-out fold.

## Milestones (each ends in a predict-then-check)
1. **Build the shared, leakage-aware CV harness and the GBLUP/RR-BLUP baseline.** Write one function that takes a fitted-predictor interface and returns mean ± SE of held-out **predictive ability** (Pearson correlation of predicted vs. observed) over K folds, on a single shared fold-assignment vector. Fit the GBLUP/RR-BLUP baseline through it first. Also build a **relatedness-aware split** (leave-cluster/family-out using the G or pedigree matrix) alongside plain k-fold. *Predict before you run: will random k-fold report higher or lower predictive ability than the relatedness-aware split, and by how much? Then check — the gap is the leakage lesson, and it is the first row of your CV results table.*
2. **Add the penalized linear contenders** (Ridge, Elastic Net) through the identical folds. *Predict before you run: where will Ridge and Elastic Net land relative to GBLUP — essentially tied (RR-BLUP ≈ Ridge), or different? Will Elastic Net's sparsity help or hurt on a polygenic trait spread across many small effects? Then check.*
3. **Add the nonlinear contenders** (Random Forest, XGBoost, small neural net), tuning each *only* inside the inner CV loop. *Predict before you run: will any tree ensemble or the neural net beat the linear baseline on held-out predictive ability? For an additive trait on 599 lines, predict they will not — and predict which method has the largest train-to-held-out gap. Then check, and note whether any apparent "win" is inside the fold-to-fold noise.*
4. **Marker-effect / feature analysis.** Compare what each model considers important: Ridge/Elastic Net coefficients, RF importances, XGBoost gain, and the RR-BLUP marker effects. If you also ran the Phase 3 GWAS on a related trait, overlay its peaks. *Predict before you look: will the markers the linear models weight most heavily agree with the tree-ensemble importances and with any GWAS hits — or will the models "disagree" about which markers matter while predicting almost equally well? Then check, and explain what agreement-or-not implies about whether marker importance is even identifiable on a polygenic trait in LD.*
5. **Error analysis + predicted-vs-observed plots and the write-up.** Make a predicted-vs-observed scatter per method (held-out predictions only), with the 1:1 line and the correlation printed. Then decompose error: where do *all* models fail together (hard-to-predict lines)? Bin held-out error by each line's relatedness to its training fold and by trait extremity. *Predict before you compute: does predictive accuracy fall as test lines become less related to the training set, and do the extreme-yield lines get pulled toward the mean? Then check — this is the relatedness mechanism made visible — and write the verdict: which method won, by how much, whether the difference survives the uncertainty, and why.*

## Deliverables
- [ ] **CV results table** — predictive ability (mean ± SE across folds) for every method × every CV scheme (random k-fold and relatedness-aware), in one table, from the shared harness.
- [ ] **Predicted-vs-observed plots** — one held-out scatter per method, 1:1 line and correlation annotated; a faceted panel makes the ranking legible at a glance.
- [ ] **Marker-effect / feature analysis** — coefficient/importance comparison across methods (and against GWAS peaks if available), with a note on whether the models agree about which markers matter.
- [ ] **Error analysis** — held-out error decomposed by relatedness-to-training and by trait extremity; the figure that shows accuracy decaying with distance from the training set.
- [ ] **Write-up** — "Why the fancy model didn't win my genomic-prediction benchmark" (or the honest opposite, if one did): the verdict, the uncertainty, and the genetic-architecture reasoning. A `post` candidate that feeds the writing loop — strong portfolio material.
- [ ] Notebook / script (committed) — shared harness + all method families + R baseline glue, fully reproducible from raw `wheat` data with fixed seeds; feeds the [[project-2-genomic-prediction-benchmark|capstone]].
- [ ] Figures are candidates for a `docs/` D3 visualization via `biostat-viz`.

## Stretch goals
- Add **BayesB** or a Bayesian-LASSO model from BGLR and contrast its variable-selection behavior with Elastic Net — does explicit sparsity help on this trait, or confirm the many-small-effects story?
- Run all four **wheat mega-environments** as separate targets and relate each trait's achievable predictive ability to its heritability; draw the connection to the heritability-as-ceiling claim.
- Vary **training-set size** to draw learning curves per method; relate the slope to heritability and the effective number of independent chromosome segments, and check whether the neural net's curve is still climbing where the linear model's has plateaued.
- Add a **genotype-by-environment** prediction (predict an unobserved line in an unobserved environment) if you use the multi-environment phenotype — this is where ML is most likely to finally earn its keep.

## Reflection prompts (Kolb RO/AC)
- What did you predict the ranking of methods would be, what actually happened, and what does the gap teach about additive vs. non-additive genetic architecture?
- Did the relatedness-aware CV split change the story versus random k-fold? What does that say about how genomic prediction "really" works — and would the difference have fooled you if you had only run k-fold?
- Where did you have to *decide* rather than *compute* (which environment as target, fold scheme, the neural net's capacity, the importance metric)? Log those judgment calls — they are the content of the write-up.
- Which evergreen claim did this confirm, extend, or contradict? If a nonlinear model *did* win, was it signal (non-additivity, G×E) or leakage — and how did you tell the difference?
