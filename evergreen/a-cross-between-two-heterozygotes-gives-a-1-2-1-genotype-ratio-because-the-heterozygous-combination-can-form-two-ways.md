# A cross between two heterozygotes gives a 1:2:1 genotype ratio because the heterozygous combination can form two ways

At one locus a parent passes just one of its two alleles to each offspring, each
with probability one-half (segregation). Crossing two heterozygotes (`CT × CT`)
therefore gives four equally likely allele combinations — `CC`, `CT`, `TC`,
`TT` — each with probability one-quarter.

Counting by genotype rather than by ordered pair: `CC` is genotype `0` and `TT`
is genotype `2`, each reachable from a single combination (25% each). But `CT`
and `TC` are the *same* genotype (order of inheritance is not tracked), so the
heterozygote, genotype `1`, collects two of the four combinations and reaches
50%. The result is the 1:2:1 ratio — 25% / 50% / 25%.

The heterozygote is the most common offspring precisely because it is the only
genotype reachable by two paths (either parent can supply either allele); each
homozygote has just one path. In breeding terms, crossing two carriers of a
variant yields about one-quarter homozygous variant, one-half carriers, and
one-quarter homozygous common — the segregation a breeder plans a cross around.
