# p2m3 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p2m3-mixed-models-blup-and-relationship-matrices|p2m3 · Mixed Models, BLUP, and Relationship Matrices]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Two claims sit at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to one of them: **(A)** BLUP predicts random breeding values by shrinking each individual toward the population mean in proportion to how little information (own records plus relatives') it carries; and **(B)** the relationship matrix is what lets information flow between relatives, and replacing the pedigree-based `A` with a marker-based `G` is exactly the step from BLUP to genomic prediction — the rest of the machinery is unchanged.

Grounded software references for the apply-level items: `rrBLUP` (Endelman 2011, *The Plant Genome* 4:250–255; functions `mixed.solve`, `A.mat`, `kin.blup`) and `sommer` (Covarrubias-Pazaran 2016, *PLOS ONE* 11(6):e0156744; `mmer`/`mmes` REML interface). Both confirmed on CRAN.

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** Write the linear mixed model in matrix form and label which term carries the breeding values and which carries the fixed effects.
   *Answer sketch:* `y = Xβ + Zu + e`. Xβ = fixed effects (e.g., overall mean, environment/year, management block); Zu = random effects, where `u` is the vector of breeding values; e = residual. Watch for putting breeding values in β.
2. **Q:** What does BLUP stand for, and what are the two key adjectives doing for you?
   *Answer sketch:* Best Linear Unbiased Prediction. "Best" = minimum mean-squared-error among linear predictors; "Unbiased" = the predictions are correct on average / don't systematically over- or under-shoot. "Prediction" (vs estimation) signals the targets are *random* effects (breeding values), not fixed parameters.
3. **Q:** What does an off-diagonal entry `A[i,j]` of the numerator relationship matrix represent? What is on the diagonal for a non-inbred individual?
   *Answer sketch:* `A[i,j]` is (twice the kinship =) the expected additive relationship between individuals i and j — the expected proportion of alleles they share identical-by-descent, scaled so full sibs ≈ 0.5, parent–offspring = 0.5. Diagonal = 1 + inbreeding coefficient (= 1 for non-inbred).

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** Why are breeding values modeled as *random* effects rather than fixed? What does treating them as random buy you?
   *Answer sketch:* They are draws from a population with a known covariance structure (Var(u) = A·σ²_A), so we can borrow that structure as a prior. Treating them as random enables shrinkage toward the mean and lets information flow between relatives through A — neither is possible if each breeding value is a free fixed parameter. (Claims A and B both depend on this.)
2. **Q:** Describe in your own words what BLUP shrinks, what it shrinks toward, and what controls *how much* a given individual is shrunk.
   *Answer sketch:* It shrinks each individual's predicted breeding value toward the population mean (≈ 0 on the deviation scale). The amount of shrinkage is set by how much information the individual carries — number and precision of its own records plus the records of its relatives (channeled through A) — and by the variance ratio (residual vs additive, i.e., heritability). Data-poor → shrunk hard; data-rich → trusted more. (Claim A.)
3. **Q:** An individual with *no phenotypic record of its own* still receives a non-trivial BLUP breeding-value prediction. Explain mechanically where that information comes from.
   *Answer sketch:* From its relatives, via the relationship matrix. A's off-diagonals set non-zero prior covariance between its breeding value and its relatives' breeding values, so their records pull its prediction off the mean. With no relatives and no record, the prediction would collapse to the mean. (Claim B — A is the conduit.)

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** You have unbalanced data: individual P has one noisy own-record; individual Q has ten recorded progeny and no own-record. Predict which one BLUP shrinks harder toward the mean and why, then say which you'd trust more as a parent.
   *Answer sketch:* P is shrunk harder — one noisy record is thin information, so BLUP discounts it toward the mean. Q, with ten progeny channeled through A, carries far more information; its prediction is shrunk less and is more reliable. Trust Q more as a parent. (Claim A.)
2. **Q:** Sketch the pipeline to fit pedigree BLUP in R: which function builds the relationship matrix from genotypes, which solves the single-variance-component mixed model, and what is the role of REML here?
   *Answer sketch:* In `rrBLUP`: `A.mat` builds a marker-based relationship matrix (for pedigree A you'd build from the pedigree, e.g., a kinship routine; A.mat is the marker version); `mixed.solve` solves the one-variance-component mixed model and returns BLUPs; `kin.blup` wraps a kinship-based fit. REML estimates the variance components (σ²_A, σ²_e) that calibrate the shrinkage — without them the variance ratio in the mixed-model equations is unknown. (`sommer`'s `mmer`/`mmes` does the multi-component REML version.) Accept correct mechanics even if exact function names are fuzzy; the *concepts* (build relationship matrix → REML for variance components → solve MME for BLUPs) are what's graded.
3. **Q:** You currently feed your BLUP model a pedigree-derived `A`. State the single change that converts this into genomic prediction (GBLUP), and name what stays exactly the same.
   *Answer sketch:* Replace `A` with a marker-based genomic relationship matrix `G` (e.g., from `A.mat` on the SNP genotypes). Everything else — the mixed model y = Xβ + Zu + e, the mixed-model equations, REML for variance components, the shrinkage logic, the BLUP solve — stays identical. (Claim B, the keystone.)

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Pedigree `A` gives the *expected* relationship between two full sibs as 0.5 for every pair. A marker-based `G` gives a different number for each full-sib pair. Why, and which is closer to the truth?
   *Answer sketch:* Mendelian sampling: full sibs inherit a random half of each parent's genome, so the *realized* fraction shared IBD varies around 0.5 (roughly ±a few percent). Pedigree A records only the *expectation*; G measures the *realized* relatedness from the markers. G is closer to truth, which is one reason GBLUP can out-predict pedigree BLUP — it distinguishes individuals the pedigree treats as identical. (Claim B's "why it can beat pedigree BLUP.")
2. **Q:** REML estimates of σ²_A and σ²_e are "just nuisance variance components." Argue against this framing using what they do inside BLUP.
   *Answer sketch:* They are not nuisances — their ratio *is* the regularization strength. The variance ratio (≈ residual/additive, tied to heritability) sets how hard BLUP shrinks: low heritability → big residual share → strong shrinkage; high heritability → light shrinkage. So h² (from p2m1) is not merely descriptive here; it is a tuning input to the predictor. Mis-estimate the variance components and you mis-tune the shrinkage. (Claim A, with the p2m1 tie-in.)
3. **Q:** Frame BLUP for an ML colleague: what familiar estimator is it, and what plays the role of the regularization penalty and of the design that couples observations?
   *Answer sketch:* BLUP is ridge-regression / a penalized (regularized) linear predictor, equivalently the posterior mean under a Gaussian prior on breeding values. The variance ratio is the ridge penalty (shrinkage strength); the relationship matrix A (or G) is the prior covariance that *couples* individuals so an observation on one informs another — like a structured prior / kernel over individuals rather than independent priors. Endelman 2011 makes the RR≡BLUP equivalence explicit. (Bridges claim A and B to ML.)

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A program has a deep, accurate pedigree but is debating whether to spend its budget genotyping the candidates for GBLUP. Under what conditions does swapping `A` for `G` buy the most accuracy, and when might it buy little? Make the call.
   *Answer sketch:* G helps most when (i) the pedigree is shallow, incomplete, or has errors; (ii) candidates have *no own phenotype* and must borrow from relatives; (iii) you need to distinguish individuals the pedigree treats as equally related (e.g., ranking within full-sib families — pedigree A can't, G can via realized relatedness and Mendelian sampling); and (iv) markers tag causal variants well (good LD/coverage). G buys little when the pedigree is already deep and accurate, relationships are far (low LD information), or marker density is too sparse to estimate realized relatedness. The judgment must weigh genotyping cost against the realized-relatedness and within-family-ranking gains. (Claim B's tradeoff.)
2. **Q:** Design an experiment, using a simulator where true breeding values are known, that demonstrates BLUP/GBLUP raises *realized selection response* relative to selecting on raw phenotype. What do you measure, and what result would confirm the module's keystone claim?
   *Answer sketch:* Generate a population with known true breeding values (variance engine from p2m1) and a selection loop (from p2m2). For the same selected fraction (fixed i), rank candidates by (a) raw phenotype, (b) pedigree BLUP using A, (c) GBLUP using G from A.mat. Measure prediction accuracy r = cor(predicted, true breeding value) and the *realized* response R per generation. Confirming result: r rises a < b < c and realized R rises with it at fixed i — because higher accuracy lifts R = i·r·σ_A (closing the loop to p2m2's "accuracy is the whole game"), and GBLUP's edge over pedigree BLUP traces to G capturing realized relatedness (claim B).

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: BLUP just averages an individual's own records; relatives are a side detail.** → **surfacing question:** "An individual with zero own-records still gets a prediction off the mean. Where did it come from, and what in the model carried it?" (Correct: relatives, via the off-diagonals of the relationship matrix — that conduit is the point of A, claim B.)
- **Trap: shrinkage is a flaw / BLUP is 'biased' and therefore worse than the naive estimate.** → **surfacing question:** "Why does deliberately pulling a noisy single-record prediction toward the mean give lower error than trusting that record at face value?" (Correct: bias-variance trade — shrinkage cuts variance more than it adds bias; BLUP minimizes MSE. The naive estimate over-fits noise.)
- **Trap: the relationship matrix is just bookkeeping; the model would work the same without it.** → **surfacing question:** "If you replaced A with the identity matrix, what would happen to information flow between relatives and to a record-less individual's prediction?" (Correct: information stops flowing — relatives become independent, the record-less individual collapses to the mean; A is what couples them, claim B.)
- **Trap: genomic prediction is a fundamentally new algorithm, unrelated to pedigree BLUP.** → **surfacing question:** "Name the one thing that changes going from pedigree BLUP to GBLUP, and the parts that stay identical." (Correct: only A→G changes; the mixed model, MME, REML, and shrinkage logic are unchanged — the keystone claim B.)
- **Trap: variance components (σ²_A, σ²_e) are just diagnostics, separate from the prediction.** → **surfacing question:** "If your REML over-estimated the residual variance, what happens to how hard BLUP shrinks every individual?" (Correct: it over-shrinks — the variance ratio *is* the regularization strength; heritability tunes the predictor, claim A.)
- **Trap: pedigree A and genomic G are interchangeable and give the same relatedness for relatives.** → **surfacing question:** "For two full-sib pairs, what does A say their relationship is, and what might G say? Why the difference?" (Correct: A says 0.5 for both; G gives pair-specific values via Mendelian sampling / realized IBD — and G's resolution is part of why it can out-predict A.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A cassava breeding program maintains a large clonally propagated germplasm collection with a partial, error-prone hand-recorded pedigree. Many promising new seedlings have *no yield trial data of their own yet* — they exist only as young plants — and the program wants to rank them now to decide which to advance to expensive multi-location trials. The lead proposes "just trust each seedling's own future single-plot record when we get it." A second breeder proposes fitting a mixed model on the relatives that *do* have trial records and, separately, genotyping every seedling with a SNP chip. Explain (1) how a mixed-model BLUP could rank seedlings that have no records of their own at all, naming the exact ingredient that makes that possible; (2) why the recorded pedigree being partial and error-prone specifically hurts pedigree BLUP and how swapping in a marker-based relationship matrix repairs it; and (3) what single change to the fitted model turns this from pedigree BLUP into genomic prediction, and one concrete reason genomic prediction should rank the *full-sib seedlings within a family* better than pedigree BLUP ever could.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) BLUP can rank record-less seedlings because the relationship matrix sets non-zero prior covariance between each seedling's breeding value and its recorded relatives' breeding values, so the relatives' trial records flow through the off-diagonals of A (or G) and pull each seedling's prediction off the mean in proportion to relatedness and the relatives' information; the ingredient is the relationship matrix (claim B), and the predictions are shrunk toward the mean according to how much relative-information each carries (claim A). (2) A partial/error-prone pedigree gives wrong or missing relationships, so pedigree A mis-routes (or fails to route) information between relatives, degrading every record-less seedling's prediction; a marker-based G is computed directly from the SNP genotypes and so measures realized relatedness without relying on the recorded pedigree, repairing the broken links and even correcting pedigree errors. (3) The single change is replacing A with the marker-based genomic relationship matrix G — everything else (the mixed model, REML variance components, the BLUP solve, the shrinkage logic) is unchanged. Genomic prediction ranks within-family full sibs better because pedigree A assigns every full-sib pair the same expected relationship (0.5) and so cannot distinguish sibs, whereas G captures the *realized* Mendelian-sampling differences in genome sharing, giving each sib a distinct, data-driven breeding-value prediction.
