# p5m1 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p5m1-breeding-pipelines-crossing-selection-cycles-field-trials|p5m1 · Breeding Pipelines: Crossing, Selection Cycles, Field Trials]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

One idea sits at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to it: **genomic selection pays off mainly by shortening the breeding cycle.** Selecting on predicted breeding values *before* phenotyping lets more selection cycles run per year, so the model's leverage is on `L` (cycle length, the denominator of `ΔG/year = i·r·σ_A / L`), not on `r` (accuracy, the numerator). Consequently **a model is valuable only insofar as it improves genetic gain per unit time, not for its per-se accuracy** — two models with identical accuracy can deliver very different gain. Readings that ground these answers: Crossa et al. (2017), *Trends in Plant Science* 22(11):961–975 (history and principle of GS, why it accelerates the cycle); the CIMMYT / CGIAR Excellence in Breeding "Basics of Genomic Selection" toolbox (the three levers — accuracy, intensity, cycle-time reduction); and CIMMYT wheat/maize implementation case studies. *(All citations verified; see manifest.)*

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** Write the breeder's equation in its rate form, with cycle length included, and define every symbol.
   *Answer sketch:* `ΔG/year = i·r·σ_A / L`. `i` = selection intensity (selection differential in phenotypic SD units); `r` = accuracy of selection (correlation between the selection criterion and true breeding value); `σ_A` = additive genetic standard deviation; `L` = cycle length, the years for one full turn of the loop (crossing → selection → recycling parents). Reject answers that drop `L` and report only `ΔG` *per cycle* — the whole module hangs on time being in the denominator. Accept `R = i·r·σ_A` as the per-cycle numerator if the learner then divides by `L`.
2. **Q:** Name the four stages of a breeding program drawn as a closed loop, and the arrow that makes it a loop rather than a pipeline.
   *Answer sketch:* Crossing (making the new families) → advancing/inbreeding the segregating generations → multi-stage, multi-environment field testing (early small-plot/few-locations → late large-plot/many-locations) → and the closing arrow: **recycling the best as parents** for the next round of crosses. The recycling arrow is what makes it a cycle; without it, time per loop would not compound.
3. **Q:** In one sentence, what decision does a genomic prediction actually make when it is inserted into the pipeline?
   *Answer sketch:* It supplies a *ranking* (predicted breeding values) used to decide which lines to advance, which to discard, and which to recycle as parents — before or instead of waiting for a full season of phenotyping. It replaces a phenotyping-based selection decision, not a measurement.

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** Why is the figure of merit *genetic gain per unit time* rather than *gain per cycle*? What does putting `L` in the denominator buy you?
   *Answer sketch:* Because the program is a loop that recycles parents, gains compound *across rounds*; what matters for the program's velocity is how much genetic improvement accrues per year, which is gain-per-cycle divided by years-per-cycle. Putting `L` in the denominator makes time a first-class lever: a scheme that turns the loop faster banks gain sooner and gets to compound it through more rounds. A bare per-cycle `ΔG` is "an orphan" — it can't be compared across schemes with different cycle lengths.
2. **Q:** Explain mechanistically *how* genomic selection shortens the cycle. Where in the loop does the time get cut, and what is the prediction substituting for?
   *Answer sketch:* GS lets you select candidates on a predicted breeding value computed from markers, *before* (or instead of) growing them out for a full season of multi-environment phenotyping. The time saved is the waiting — seasons/sites of plant growth and measurement that a phenotypic scheme needs before it can rank candidates. The prediction substitutes for that wait: you can recycle a selected line as a parent a generation (or several) earlier, sometimes running more than one cycle per year (rapid cycling / fast generation advance). The lever is on `L`, not on the per-se quality of any single ranking.
3. **Q:** Two breeders both adopt "a genomic prediction model." One uses it to make the *same* selection decision one year sooner; the other uses it only to slightly re-rank candidates at the existing late stage, leaving the timeline unchanged. Which one expects more genetic gain per year, and why does that distinguish the two uses?
   *Answer sketch:* The first. Pulling the decision forward shrinks `L` (the denominator), which compounds across rounds; the second only nudges `r` (numerator) at fixed `L`, a much smaller and non-compounding effect. Same model, two deployments, very different gain — which is exactly why "model value" must be measured as gain per unit time, not accuracy in isolation (core claim).

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** A conventional scheme has `i` = 1.5, `r` = 0.6, `σ_A` = 1.0, `L` = 4 years. A genomic scheme keeps `i` and `σ_A` the same but selects on predicted breeding values one stage earlier: `L` drops to 2 years, but acting earlier on a prediction lowers accuracy to `r` = 0.5. Compute `ΔG/year` for both and say which wins.
   *Answer sketch:* Conventional: `ΔG/year = 1.5·0.6·1.0 / 4 = 0.225`. Genomic: `1.5·0.5·1.0 / 2 = 0.375`. The genomic scheme wins (0.375 vs 0.225, ~67% more gain per year) *despite lower accuracy*, because halving `L` more than offsets the accuracy drop. This is the core claim made arithmetic.
2. **Q:** Using the same conventional baseline (`i`=1.5, `r`=0.6, `σ_A`=1.0, `L`=4), find the break-even accuracy: how low can the genomic scheme's `r` fall, at `L`=2, before it stops beating the conventional scheme?
   *Answer sketch:* Set `i·r_B·σ_A / 2 = i·0.6·σ_A / 4`. The `i·σ_A` cancels: `r_B / 2 = 0.6 / 4 = 0.15`, so `r_B = 0.30`. The genomic scheme wins for any accuracy above 0.30 — i.e. it can lose half the accuracy and still come out ahead because it halved the time. The reflex to build: accuracy and time trade against each other inside one quantity.
3. **Q:** A vendor pitches a model that is "5% more accurate" but plugs into the existing pipeline with no change to cycle length. Quantify the gain it buys relative to a change that instead halves `L` at unchanged accuracy. Use any reasonable baseline.
   *Answer sketch:* A 5% relative accuracy bump scales `ΔG/year` by ×1.05 (e.g. +5%). Halving `L` scales it by ×2 (+100%). The cycle-time move is ~20× the gain of the accuracy move. Numerically obvious once both are in `ΔG/year = i·r·σ_A / L`: `r` is linear in the numerator, `L` is linear in the denominator, and the realistic room to move `L` (halving) dwarfs the realistic room to move `r` (a few percent). Direct application of the core claim.

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Two models report *identical* cross-validated accuracy `r` on the same data, yet deliver different genetic gain per year when deployed. Give two distinct mechanisms by which that happens.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) **Cycle position / timing:** one model's prediction is usable early enough to recycle parents sooner (smaller `L`), the other only at a late stage (large `L`) — same `r`, different denominator. (2) **What it enables operationally:** one supports running two cycles per year or a larger candidate pool at the selection step (raising `i` or enabling rapid cycling), the other does not. Accuracy is necessary but not sufficient; the deployment context (which decision, when, how often) sets the gain. The lesson: an accuracy number with no cycle attached is an orphan.
2. **Q:** List the levers in `ΔG/year = i·r·σ_A / L` and rank, with justification, which one genomic selection most plausibly moves and which is most constrained in practice.
   *Answer sketch:* Levers: `i` (intensity — diminishing returns, and pushing it depletes `σ_A` via inbreeding), `r` (accuracy — bounded above by 1 and by training-population relatedness/marker density; realistic moves are a few percent), `σ_A` (additive variance — a property of the germplasm, erodes under selection, not a free dial), `L` (cycle length — often *halvable* by predicting and recycling earlier). GS most plausibly and most powerfully moves `L`; `r` is real but small and bounded; `i` and `σ_A` are constrained or self-limiting. Hence the claim that GS "pays off mainly by shortening the cycle."
3. **Q:** A validation reports a high random-hold-out accuracy, and a breeder buys the model on that number. Why might that accuracy *overstate* the gain the model will deliver, and what validation design would mirror real use?
   *Answer sketch:* A random hold-out leaks information: relatives and same-environment data sit on both sides of the split, inflating `r` relative to how the model is actually used (predicting *not-yet-phenotyped* lines, often in new years/environments, to decide advancement). It also says nothing about whether the model lets you act *sooner* — the value driver. A faithful design mirrors deployment: predict lines/cycles the model would actually have to call before phenotyping (e.g. forward prediction across years, or predicting the next cycle's untested candidates), and then judge the scheme by `ΔG/year`, not pooled `r`.

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A program has a fixed annual budget. Option A: spend it densifying genotyping to raise model accuracy from `r`=0.55 to `r`=0.62 at the current `L`=4. Option B: spend it on a rapid-cycling facility (off-season nursery / speed breeding) that drops `L` from 4 to 2 with accuracy unchanged. Judge which buys more genetic gain per year, and name the condition under which your answer flips.
   *Answer sketch:* Compute ratios. A: `r` ×(0.62/0.55) ≈ ×1.13 (+13%). B: `L` halved ≈ ×2 (+100%). B dominates by a wide margin. The answer flips only if the cycle-shortening forces a *large* accuracy collapse or a *large* loss of `σ_A`/intensity — e.g. if predicting that much earlier dropped `r` below the break-even (here, below ~0.55/2 = 0.275, far below realistic) or if the off-season environment so degrades selection that `i·r·σ_A` more than halves. A strong answer states the break-even explicitly and notes that, realistically, B almost always wins — which is the module's whole point.
2. **Q:** Design a simulation that isolates the contribution of *cycle-time reduction* to genetic gain, holding accuracy, intensity, and additive variance fixed. What do you vary, hold fixed, and measure — and over what horizon?
   *Answer sketch:* Hold `i`, `r`, `σ_A`, the generative genetics, and the selected fraction identical across arms. Vary only `L` (e.g. 4, 3, 2, 1 years per cycle), implemented as how soon a selected line is recycled as a parent. Measure cumulative genetic gain over a *fixed wall-clock horizon* (say 10 years), not per cycle — so the faster-cycling arms simply complete more loops. Expect cumulative gain to rise as `L` falls, roughly proportional to cycles-completed, demonstrating that time, at fixed accuracy, is the dominant lever (core claim). A strong answer notes the horizon must be in years, and that holding `σ_A` truly fixed requires either re-injecting variance or limiting the horizon so depletion doesn't confound the cycle effect.

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: a better model is a more accurate model; accuracy is the goal.** → **surfacing question:** "I have two models with identical cross-validated accuracy. Can one deliver twice the genetic gain per year as the other? How?" (Correct: yes — via where in the loop it acts and how much it shortens `L`; accuracy without a cycle attached is an orphan.)
- **Trap: genomic selection helps mainly because the genomic prediction is more accurate than phenotype.** → **surfacing question:** "If a genomic prediction were *slightly less* accurate than full phenotyping, could adopting it still raise genetic gain per year? When?" (Correct: yes — if acting on it earlier shortens `L` enough, the time saved outweighs the accuracy lost; GS's main payoff is cycle-time, not accuracy.)
- **Trap: genetic gain is the per-cycle response `R`; report `R` and you're done.** → **surfacing question:** "Two schemes both deliver `R` = 0.4 per cycle, but one cycle takes 4 years and the other 2. Are they equally good? What's the right figure of merit?" (Correct: no — divide by `L`; the 2-year scheme delivers twice the gain per year. The figure of merit is `ΔG/year`.)
- **Trap: the breeding program is a one-pass pipeline (cross once, test, release).** → **surfacing question:** "Where do next year's parents come from? Why does that arrow change which scheme variable matters most?" (Correct: the best progeny are recycled as parents — it's a closed loop, so *time per loop* compounds across rounds, making `L` the compounding lever.)
- **Trap: to lift gain, just select harder (push intensity).** → **surfacing question:** "Why might pushing intensity from the top 10% to the top 1% buy little extra gain, while halving the cycle buys a lot — and what does extreme intensity cost the program?" (Correct: `i` has diminishing returns and depletes `σ_A` via inbreeding; `L` is the more powerful and less self-defeating lever for a recycling program.)
- **Trap: a high random-hold-out accuracy proves the model will deliver gain.** → **surfacing question:** "Your model scored `r`=0.8 on a random hold-out. Why might that overstate the gain it delivers, and what would you validate instead?" (Correct: random hold-out leaks relatedness/environment and ignores *timing*; validate by forward/earlier prediction mirroring real use and judge by `ΔG/year`.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A cassava breeding program is the unfamiliar case here: cassava is a clonally propagated root crop with a long, slow cycle — a plant must be grown to maturity, its storage roots harvested and evaluated, and only then can the best clones be multiplied (slowly, from stem cuttings) and recycled, so one conventional selection cycle runs roughly 5 years. A donor offers funding for exactly one of two things. Option 1: a high-density genotyping platform plus a model that, on the program's own random-hold-out, predicts root yield with accuracy `r` = 0.65 — but in this program a genomic prediction could only be acted on at the *same* late stage as now, after roots are evaluated, so the 5-year cycle is unchanged. Option 2: the same genotyping platform feeding a model with a *lower* hold-out accuracy of `r` = 0.50, but used to predict and select *seedling-stage* clones from markers alone — before the multi-year root evaluation — letting selected clones be multiplied and recycled as parents at year 2 instead of year 5, and supporting two overlapping cycles in the time one used to take. Intensity and additive variance are the same under both. Diagnose which option a breeder optimizing genetic gain per unit time should fund, show the reasoning with the rate form of the breeder's equation, state the break-even accuracy below which your recommendation would flip, and name two ways the seductive Option-1 accuracy number could mislead.
   *Answer sketch:* Fund **Option 2**. Use `ΔG/year = i·r·σ_A / L` with `i·σ_A` common to both. Option 1: `r`/`L` = 0.65/5 = 0.13. Option 2 (cycle to ~2 years): 0.50/2 = 0.25 — nearly double the gain per year, *despite* the lower accuracy, because the cycle was shortened. Break-even: set `r_B / 2 = 0.65 / 5`, giving `r_B = 0.26`; Option 2 wins for any accuracy above ~0.26, so it has enormous headroom. The trap is treating `r`=0.65 as "the better model": (1) it is an orphan accuracy with no cycle shortening attached, so it scales gain by `r` alone at a large `L`; (2) the 0.65 is a *random hold-out* in a clonal program where relatives and shared environments inflate it, overstating the gain it would actually deliver on not-yet-evaluated seedlings — exactly the situations where Option 2 is judged. Strong answers add: the dominant lever for a slow clonal crop is cutting `L` (seedling-stage genomic selection / rapid recycling), not chasing a few accuracy points; and the program should be validated by forward prediction of untested seedlings and judged by `ΔG/year`, not pooled `r`. This is the core claim transferred intact: GS pays off mainly by shortening the cycle, and a model is worth only the gain-per-year it enables.
