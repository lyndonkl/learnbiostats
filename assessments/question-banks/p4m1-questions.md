# p4m1 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p4m1-classical-genomic-prediction-gblup-and-rr-blup|p4m1 · Classical Genomic Prediction: GBLUP and RR-BLUP]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Two claims sit at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to one of them: **(A)** genomic prediction works because markers in linkage disequilibrium with causal variants let the genomic relationship matrix `G` estimate *realized* relatedness far more precisely than a pedigree, and realized relatedness is what actually predicts an unphenotyped individual; and **(B)** RR-BLUP on markers and GBLUP on the `G` matrix are the same model viewed two ways — estimating many shrunk marker effects and estimating line effects under a marker-derived covariance produce equivalent genetic-value predictions.

Grounded references for apply-level items: the founding paper is Meuwissen, Hayes & Goddard (2001), *Genetics* 157(4):1819–1829 (estimate *all* marker effects simultaneously, with shrinkage/Bayesian framing). Software: `rrBLUP` (Endelman 2011, *The Plant Genome* 4(3):250–255; `mixed.solve`, `A.mat`, `kin.blup`) and `BGLR` (Pérez & de los Campos 2014, *Genetics* 198(2):483–495; ships the `wheat` dataset — 599 CIMMYT lines × 1,279 markers). The plant-breeding review is Crossa et al. (2017), *Trends in Plant Science* 22(11):961–975. All confirmed.

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** What was the central methodological proposal of Meuwissen, Hayes & Goddard (2001) that founded genomic selection — what did it do differently from marker-assisted selection?
   *Answer sketch:* Estimate the effects of *all* markers across the whole genome simultaneously and predict total genetic value from the lot — rather than first testing markers for significance and using only the few "significant" ones (MAS). The trick that makes fitting thousands of effects from limited records possible is shrinkage / a Bayesian prior on effect sizes. Watch for confusing GS with GWAS or MAS.
2. **Q:** What does the acronym GEBV stand for, and how is a GEBV different from a phenotype?
   *Answer sketch:* Genomic Estimated Breeding Value — the predicted *additive genetic merit* of an individual estimated from genome-wide markers. A phenotype is genotype + environment + noise; a GEBV is an estimate of just the heritable, transmissible part, with the environmental noise modeled out.
3. **Q:** Write down one common formula for the genomic relationship matrix `G`, and label each piece.
   *Answer sketch:* `G = ZZ' / k`, where `Z` is the centered (and usually scaled) genotype matrix (lines × markers, alleles coded 0/1/2 then centered by allele frequency), `Z'` its transpose, and `k` a scaling constant (commonly `2·Σ p_j(1−p_j)`, twice the sum of allele-frequency variances). The cross-product `ZZ'` is over markers, giving a line × line matrix. Accept the VanRaden form; the load-bearing point is cross-product of centered genotypes, scaled.
4. **Q:** In RR-BLUP, how many effects are estimated and what is done to them; in GBLUP, what does the model estimate instead?
   *Answer sketch:* RR-BLUP estimates one (shrunk, ridge-penalized) effect per marker — so ~one per column of the genotype matrix. GBLUP never estimates a marker effect; it estimates one random effect (breeding value) per *line*, with covariance among lines given by `G`. (Claim B: same model, two parameterizations.)

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** Why is shrinkage not optional in RR-BLUP — what specifically goes wrong if you fit one effect per marker by ordinary least squares?
   *Answer sketch:* There are far more markers than individuals (e.g., 1,279 markers, 599 lines). With more parameters than observations OLS is under-determined / over-fits — it fits the training noise exactly and generalizes terribly. Ridge shrinkage caps the effect sizes, trading a little bias for a large variance reduction, which is what makes estimating thousands of small additive effects from limited records feasible at all. (Ties to Meuwissen 2001's motivation.)
2. **Q:** GBLUP never estimates a single marker effect, yet it predicts genetic merit from markers. Where did the marker information go?
   *Answer sketch:* Into `G`. The markers are used only to *measure relatedness* — every marker's contribution is summed into the line × line covariance `G = ZZ'/k`. The model then predicts each line's breeding value by borrowing from related lines through `G`, exactly as pedigree BLUP borrows through `A`. The information is carried in the covariance, not in named effects. (Claim B; Claim A's "relatedness is the carrier.")
3. **Q:** State the equivalence between RR-BLUP and GBLUP in one or two sentences, including the equation that links the two views.
   *Answer sketch:* They are the same model. Marker effects `u` (RR-BLUP) map to line genetic values `g` via `g = Z·u` — the genotype matrix projects the per-marker effects onto the per-line values. Because `G = ZZ'/k` is built from that same `Z`, fitting line effects under covariance `G` and fitting shrunk marker effects yield identical predicted genetic values (under matched variance assumptions). (Claim B, the keystone.)
4. **Q:** A pedigree says two full sibs are related by exactly 0.5. A dense-marker `G` gives a slightly different number for each full-sib pair. Why?
   *Answer sketch:* Mendelian sampling — each sib inherits an independent random half of each parent's genome, so the *realized* fraction of genome they actually share varies around the 0.5 expectation. The pedigree records only the expectation (0.5 for all full sibs); `G` measures the realized sharing from the markers, so it distinguishes pairs the pedigree treats as identical. (Claim A.)

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** Walk through, step by step, how you would build a `G` matrix from a raw 0/1/2 genotype matrix, and say what each step is for.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) Code alleles 0/1/2 (allele dosage). (2) Center each marker column by subtracting `2p_j` (its allele-frequency-implied mean) so the mean dosage is removed → `Z`. (3) Optionally scale. (4) Cross-product `ZZ'` to get pairwise similarity summed across markers. (5) Divide by `k = 2·Σ p_j(1−p_j)` so `G` is on the same scale as the pedigree `A`. Centering removes the mean so the matrix measures *deviation* sharing; scaling by `k` makes diagonals average ~1, comparable to `A`.
2. **Q:** You fit RR-BLUP and GBLUP on the same wheat dataset and scatter-plot the predicted genetic values from each against the other. What should the plot look like, and what would it mean if the points did *not* lie on the identity line?
   *Answer sketch:* Points should fall essentially on the identity line — the two parameterizations give the same predictions (Claim B). If they diverge materially, you have a mismatch: most often the variance assumptions differ (e.g., a different ridge penalty than the implied `σ²_g/σ²_e` in GBLUP), or `G` was built with a different centering/scaling than the `Z` used in RR-BLUP. The equivalence holds only when the implied shrinkage matches.
3. **Q:** You hold out 20% of lines (mask their phenotypes), predict them from the rest, and correlate predicted with observed. Should that held-out correlation be near 1.0 or well below it, and what caps it?
   *Answer sketch:* Well below 1.0. The ceiling is set by heritability (you can only predict the genetic part) *and* by how well the training lines' relatedness and the markers' LD with causal variants let you reconstruct each held-out line's genetic value. For a trait like wheat yield this is commonly in the ~0.3–0.6 predictive-ability range, not ~1.0. A learner guessing ~1.0 has missed both the h² ceiling and the relatedness dependence.
4. **Q:** Name the single change to a pedigree-BLUP pipeline that converts it into GBLUP, and what stays identical.
   *Answer sketch:* Replace the pedigree-based numerator relationship matrix `A` with the marker-based genomic relationship matrix `G`. Everything else — the mixed model `y = Xβ + Zu + e`, the mixed-model equations, REML for variance components, the BLUP solve, the shrinkage logic — is unchanged. (Bridges to p2m3; Claim B in its broader form.)

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** You ran two experiments: (a) thin the markers to a random 10% subset; (b) swap genomic `G` for pedigree `A`. One hurts held-out prediction much more than the other. Predict which, and explain what that tells you about where the predictive signal lives.
   *Answer sketch:* Swapping to pedigree `A` typically hurts more than thinning to 10% of markers. Reason: predictive signal lives in the *realized relatedness* the markers reconstruct, not in the raw count of markers. Even a 10% random subset (still hundreds of markers in LD across the genome) reconstructs relatedness fairly well, so prediction degrades only modestly; but `A` discards realized relatedness entirely and uses only pedigree expectations, losing the Mendelian-sampling resolution that distinguishes relatives. This is direct evidence for Claim A: markers matter because they measure relatedness, and that is what predicts.
2. **Q:** Genomic prediction is sometimes described as "a regression on markers." Argue why "a model of realized relatedness" is the more accurate framing, using the GBLUP view.
   *Answer sketch:* In GBLUP no marker effect is ever estimated, yet prediction works — so the operative object cannot be the marker effects themselves; it is `G`, the realized-relatedness covariance. Markers enter only as the *instrument that measures* relatedness precisely (Claim A). The RR-BLUP "regression on markers" view is mathematically equivalent (Claim B) but obscures the mechanism: the model predicts an unphenotyped line by borrowing from how genetically similar it actually is to phenotyped lines.
3. **Q:** Why does `G` predicting better than `A` depend on linkage disequilibrium? What would happen to genomic prediction if markers were in *no* LD with causal variants?
   *Answer sketch:* Markers are (mostly) not themselves causal; they predict because they are correlated (in LD) with the unobserved causal variants, so sharing marker patterns tracks sharing causal alleles. If markers had zero LD with causal variants, `G` would measure relatedness at neutral loci unlinked to the trait — it would still capture *genome-wide* relatedness (useful for closely related individuals via overall IBD) but would lose the locus-specific signal, and prediction in distantly related material would collapse. LD is the bridge from "shared markers" to "shared causal effects." (Claim A's mechanism.)
4. **Q:** Where, in the equivalence `g = Z·u`, does the assumption live that every marker contributes the same prior variance — and which Bayesian alternatives (BayesA/B) relax it?
   *Answer sketch:* RR-BLUP/GBLUP assume a *common* variance for all marker effects (homogeneous shrinkage) — that is what makes ridge ≡ GBLUP. The assumption is baked into treating `u` as iid with one variance, so `Var(g) = ZZ'σ²_u ∝ G`. BayesA gives each marker its own variance (heavier-tailed, so large effects shrink less); BayesB additionally lets a fraction of markers have zero effect (variable selection). These help when a few loci have large effects (non-infinitesimal architecture); they collapse toward GBLUP when the trait is truly infinitesimal/additive. (From Meuwissen 2001 and BGLR.)

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A breeding program has a deep, accurate, multi-generation pedigree and is debating whether to spend its budget genotyping candidates for GBLUP. Under what conditions does swapping `A` for `G` buy the most accuracy, and when might it buy little? Make the call.
   *Answer sketch:* `G` buys most when (i) the pedigree is shallow, incomplete, or error-prone; (ii) candidates have no own phenotype and must borrow from relatives; (iii) you need to rank *within* full-sib families (pedigree `A` gives every full-sib pair 0.5 and cannot distinguish them; `G` resolves realized relatedness — Claim A); and (iv) markers tag causal variants well (adequate density and LD). It buys little when the pedigree is already deep and accurate, when relationships are distant (low LD-based information), or when marker density is too sparse to estimate realized relatedness. The decision weighs genotyping cost against the realized-relatedness and within-family resolution gains.
2. **Q:** Design a minimal experiment on a real dataset that would *convince a skeptic* that RR-BLUP and GBLUP are the same model, and separately that `G` beats `A`. What do you fit, plot, and measure?
   *Answer sketch:* Equivalence demo: fit RR-BLUP and GBLUP on identical data with matched variance assumptions; scatter predicted genetic values against each other — they should fall on the identity line (correlation ≈ 1.0). Optionally confirm `g = Zu` numerically by reconstructing line values from marker effects. `G`-beats-`A` demo: with the same K-fold held-out scheme, fit GBLUP twice — once with marker `G`, once with pedigree `A` built from the same families — and compare held-out predictive ability (predicted-vs-observed correlation); confirming result is `G` ≥ `A`, with the gap largest for within-family ranking and for lines whose pedigree is thin. The first plot proves Claim B; the second isolates Claim A.

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: genomic prediction is just GWAS — find the significant markers and predict from those.** → **surfacing question:** "Meuwissen 2001 fit *all* markers, not just significant ones. Why would selecting only significant markers and predicting from them do *worse* than keeping all of them shrunk?" (Correct: complex traits are many small effects, most individually non-significant; discarding them throws away most of the genetic variance. GS keeps all markers and shrinks; GWAS-then-predict is MAS and underperforms.)
- **Trap: RR-BLUP and GBLUP are different methods that happen to give similar answers.** → **surfacing question:** "Write the equation linking marker effects to line genetic values. Given that equation and `G = ZZ'/k`, are the two models similar, or identical?" (Correct: identical — `g = Zu` and `G = ZZ'/k` are the same `Z`; under matched variances the predictions are equal, not merely close. Claim B.)
- **Trap: more markers always mean more accuracy; prediction is about marker count.** → **surfacing question:** "You thin to 10% of markers and lose almost nothing, but swap `G` for pedigree `A` and lose a lot. What does that say about whether markers predict, or relatedness predicts?" (Correct: relatedness predicts; markers are just how it is measured precisely. Claim A.)
- **Trap: `G` predicts because markers are causal.** → **surfacing question:** "Most genotyped SNPs are not the causal variant. So why does sharing them predict a trait at all?" (Correct: linkage disequilibrium — markers are correlated with causal variants, so shared markers track shared causal alleles. No LD, no locus-specific prediction. Claim A's mechanism.)
- **Trap: pedigree `A` and genomic `G` give the same relatedness for relatives, so genotyping is wasteful if you have a pedigree.** → **surfacing question:** "For three different full-sib pairs, what does `A` say their relationship is, and what might `G` say? Why does that difference matter for ranking sibs?" (Correct: `A` says 0.5 for all; `G` gives pair-specific realized values via Mendelian sampling, so only `G` can rank within a family.)
- **Trap: held-out predictive ability should approach 1.0 if the method is good.** → **surfacing question:** "What are the two separate ceilings that cap held-out predictive ability below 1.0, even for a perfect estimator?" (Correct: heritability — you can only predict the genetic part — and the LD/relatedness limit on how well markers reconstruct a held-out line's genetic value.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A cassava program maintains thousands of clonally propagated genotypes. The pedigree is partial and hand-recorded with known errors, and a large batch of new seedlings has *no yield-trial data of its own* — they exist only as young plants the program must rank now to decide which to advance to expensive multi-location trials. Cassava is highly heterozygous and outcrossing, so full-sib families segregate a great deal. The lead breeder proposes ranking seedlings on their first single-plot yield once it exists; a second breeder proposes genotyping every seedling with a SNP panel and fitting a genomic model on the relatives that already have trial records. Answer: (1) How can a genomic model rank seedlings that have no records of their own, and what is the exact ingredient that makes it possible? (2) Why does the partial, error-prone pedigree specifically damage a pedigree-BLUP ranking, and how does a marker-based relationship matrix repair it? (3) Give one concrete reason a genomic model should rank *full-sib seedlings within the same family* better than any pedigree-based model ever could, naming the genetic phenomenon involved. (4) State which single change converts the pedigree model into the genomic one and what stays identical, and name the property of the markers (relative to causal variants) without which the whole scheme would fail.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) The genomic relationship matrix `G` sets non-zero prior covariance between each record-less seedling's breeding value and its recorded relatives', so the relatives' trial data flow through the off-diagonals of `G` and pull each seedling's GEBV off the population mean in proportion to realized relatedness; the ingredient is the relationship matrix (Claim A/B), and predictions are shrunk toward the mean per how much relative-information each carries. (2) A partial/error-prone pedigree mis-routes or fails to route information between relatives, so pedigree `A` is wrong and every record-less seedling's prediction degrades; `G` is computed directly from the SNP genotypes, measuring realized relatedness without trusting the recorded pedigree — it repairs broken links and even corrects pedigree errors. (3) Pedigree `A` assigns every full-sib pair exactly 0.5 and cannot tell sibs apart; in a highly heterozygous outcrosser the realized genome sharing among sibs scatters widely around 0.5 due to Mendelian sampling, and `G` captures that realized sharing, giving each sib a distinct data-driven GEBV — so only `G` can rank within a family. (4) The single change is replacing `A` with the marker-based `G`; the mixed model, REML variance components, BLUP solve, and shrinkage logic are all unchanged (Claim B). The indispensable marker property is linkage disequilibrium with the causal variants — without LD, shared markers would not track shared causal alleles and locus-specific prediction would collapse (Claim A).
