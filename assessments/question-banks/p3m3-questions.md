# p3m3 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Anchored to the module reading: **Marees et al. (2018), "A tutorial on conducting genome-wide association studies: Quality control and statistical analysis," *International Journal of Methods in Psychiatric Research* 27(2):e1608** (companion code at github.com/MareesAT/GWA_tutorial, using PLINK/PRSice/R), plus the PLINK association docs (`--assoc`, `--linear`/`--logistic` with `--covar`). Core claim under test: **GWAS tests each marker for association with a trait while correcting for relatedness and structure, and the QQ plot is the honesty check — inflation means the correction failed before any peak can be trusted.**

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** What does a single-marker association test actually test, and how many of them does a genome-wide scan run?
   *Answer sketch:* Each test asks whether genotype at *one* marker is statistically associated with the trait (e.g. regress trait on additive genotype dosage 0/1/2). A genome-wide scan runs one such test per marker — hundreds of thousands to millions of separate single-marker tests, not one joint model.
2. **Q:** What is plotted on the two axes of a QQ plot in GWAS?
   *Answer sketch:* Observed test-statistic / -log10(p-value) quantiles (y) against the quantiles expected under the null of no association (x). Points are the ranked p-values from the scan against their expected ranks.
3. **Q:** What is the genomic inflation factor lambda, and what value indicates a well-behaved scan?
   *Answer sketch:* Lambda is the ratio of the median observed test statistic to the median expected under the null (median chi-square observed / expected). Lambda ≈ 1 means well-behaved; lambda > 1 indicates systematic inflation (structure/relatedness not corrected); < 1 indicates deflation/over-correction.
4. **Q:** What two standard corrections for population structure and relatedness does the module name?
   *Answer sketch:* (1) Principal-component covariates (top PCs added as fixed-effect covariates in the regression). (2) A linear mixed model with a kinship / genomic-relationship random effect. The mixed model additionally captures relatedness between close individuals that broad PCs miss.
5. **Q:** What is the conventional genome-wide significance threshold in human GWAS, and what is a Manhattan plot?
   *Answer sketch:* ~5×10⁻⁸ (Bonferroni for ~1 million effectively-independent variants at family-wise α = 0.05). A Manhattan plot shows -log10(p) per marker along genomic position; "peaks" rise above the genome-wide significance line.

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** If most markers genuinely have no effect on the trait, what should the *bulk* of points in a QQ plot look like, and why?
   *Answer sketch:* They should hug the diagonal (y = x): under the true null, p-values are uniformly distributed, so observed quantiles match expected. Only a few real-signal markers at the extreme tail should lift above the line. A diagonal bulk = well-calibrated test.
2. **Q:** Population structure is called a *confounder* in GWAS. Explain the mechanism: how does ancestry that correlates with both the trait and a genomic region manufacture a false association?
   *Answer sketch:* If subpopulations differ in trait mean *and* in allele frequency at many markers (which they always do), then any marker whose frequency tracks ancestry will correlate with the trait through ancestry, not causation. The marker is a proxy for "which subpopulation," and the trait difference is between subpopulations — classic confounding. This happens for whole regions at once, which is why uncorrected scans inflate globally.
3. **Q:** You add PCs as covariates and lambda drops toward 1. In your own words, what did the PCs *absorb* that was previously charged to the markers?
   *Answer sketch:* The PCs soaked up the trait variation attributable to ancestry/structure. Previously, that ancestry-linked variation had nowhere to go in a marker-only model, so it leaked into every ancestry-correlated marker's effect estimate, inflating them all. With PCs in the model, ancestry is explained by the PCs and the markers are tested for their *residual* association — so the systematic inflation collapses.
4. **Q:** A mixed model adds a random effect built from the kinship matrix. What does it do that fixed PC covariates do not?
   *Answer sketch:* PC covariates capture *broad* axes of ancestry (population-level). The kinship random effect models the full pairwise relatedness covariance among individuals, so it absorbs *fine-grained* relatedness — pairs of close relatives, cryptic family structure — that no small set of global PCs represents. It corrects sample-level non-independence, not just group-level ancestry.
5. **Q:** Why does testing hundreds of thousands of markers force a threshold far stricter than p < 0.05?
   *Answer sketch:* At α = 0.05 per test, you expect 5% of *null* markers to be "significant" by chance — across, say, 500,000 markers that is ~25,000 false positives. To control the family-wise error rate you divide α by the number of (effectively independent) tests (Bonferroni), giving the genome-wide threshold (~5×10⁻⁸ for ~1M independent variants). Otherwise the genome would be littered with chance hits.

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** You run a naive association test (no covariates) on a structured dataset, make a QQ plot, and get lambda = 1.6. Then you add the top 10 PCs and lambda = 1.04. Interpret both numbers and say which scan you would interpret peaks from.
   *Answer sketch:* Lambda 1.6 = strong systematic inflation; the whole scan is lifted off the null, so even apparent peaks are untrustworthy — structure is leaking in everywhere. Lambda 1.04 = essentially calibrated; the PC correction worked. You interpret peaks only from the corrected scan, and only after confirming the QQ bulk hugs the diagonal.
2. **Q:** A naive Manhattan plot shows a tall "peak" that shrinks to nothing after PC correction. What was that peak actually marking?
   *Answer sketch:* Not a causal locus — it was a region whose allele frequencies track ancestry, where ancestry also correlates with the trait. The "signal" was the confounding ancestry path, distributed across a whole region. Correction removed the ancestry route, so the spurious peak collapses. A real causal peak would survive correction.
3. **Q:** You scan 500,000 markers. Using a Bonferroni family-wise α of 0.05, what per-marker threshold do you set, and how does that compare to the human ~5×10⁻⁸ convention?
   *Answer sketch:* 0.05 / 500,000 = 1×10⁻⁷. That is less strict than 5×10⁻⁸ because 5×10⁻⁸ corresponds to ~1,000,000 effectively-independent tests (0.05/1e6 = 5e-8); with 500k markers (and LD making them non-independent) your effective threshold lands around 1×10⁻⁷. Looking for the division and awareness that the count is *effective* independent tests, not raw marker count.
4. **Q:** Predict what happens to lambda and to your top peak if you deliberately leave in a pair of close relatives that should have been pruned, *even after* PC correction. How would you then fix it?
   *Answer sketch:* Relatedness that PCs do not capture can still inflate the scan, so lambda may stay above 1 and a spurious peak driven by the shared segments of the relative pair can persist. Fix: either prune the relatives (back to p3m2) or switch to a mixed model whose kinship random effect absorbs the pairwise relatedness directly. This is the case for the mixed model, discovered rather than asserted.

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Someone shows you a Manhattan plot with a clean genome-wide-significant peak but does not show the QQ plot. Why is the peak uninterpretable, and what exactly are you withholding judgment on?
   *Answer sketch:* Without the QQ plot/lambda you cannot tell whether the *entire* scan is inflated. If the bulk is lifted off the diagonal (lambda ≫ 1), the "significant" peak may just be the tallest mirage in a globally-inflated scan — structure not corrected. You withhold judgment on whether the test was *calibrated at all*; significance is meaningful only relative to a well-behaved null. The QQ plot is the precondition for reading any peak.
2. **Q:** Compare PC-covariate correction and the mixed model on a dataset with (a) broad continental ancestry differences and (b) several pairs of siblings. Which correction handles which problem, and where does each fail alone?
   *Answer sketch:* PCs handle (a) broad ancestry well but do not capture (b) pairwise sib relatedness — sibs are not a population axis. The mixed model's kinship random effect handles (b) and, with enough structure in the GRM, much of (a) too, but a poorly-specified kinship matrix or unmodeled discrete strata can leave residual structure. Best practice often combines them (PCs as fixed covariates inside the mixed model). The analysis point: ancestry and relatedness are different non-independence problems needing the right tool.
3. **Q:** Lambda comes out at 0.92 after you add 20 PCs. Is "lambda below 1 is even better" correct? What assumption would over-correction violate?
   *Answer sketch:* No — lambda < 1 signals *deflation* (over-correction). Throwing in too many PCs (or an over-fit mixed model) can absorb real genetic signal along with confounding, shrinking true associations and costing power. The assumption violated is that the correction removes only confounding variance; over-correction also removes legitimate trait-associated variance. The target is lambda ≈ 1, not "as low as possible."
4. **Q:** The module says GWAS is "many single-marker tests, not one joint model." What limitation of GWAS does that design choice create, and how does it interact with LD?
   *Answer sketch:* Because each marker is tested marginally, GWAS does not jointly model markers — so a single causal variant lights up *all* markers in LD with it (a peak is a region, not a SNP), and correlated markers are tested redundantly, complicating the "number of independent tests" used for the threshold. It also cannot, by itself, distinguish the causal variant from its LD neighbors (that needs fine-mapping). Connects forward: joint/whole-genome modeling is the genomic-prediction view in Phase 4.

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A collaborator reports "47 genome-wide significant hits, lambda = 1.9" and wants to publish the hit list. Evaluate the result and state what you would require before believing any hit.
   *Answer sketch:* Reject the hit list as-is. Lambda 1.9 means the scan is massively inflated — the correction failed, so the 47 "hits" are very likely an artifact of uncorrected structure/relatedness, not biology. Require: re-run with proper structure correction (more/appropriate PCs and/or a mixed model), confirm lambda ≈ 1 and a QQ bulk on the diagonal, verify relatedness pruning was done, and only then interpret whichever peaks survive. Honesty check before interpretation is the whole discipline of this module.
2. **Q:** Design the figure-and-number package you would attach to *every* GWAS to let a reader judge it honestly, and justify each element.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) QQ plot — shows whether the null bulk is calibrated. (2) Lambda — single-number inflation summary. (3) Manhattan plot with the genome-wide significance line drawn. (4) The exact threshold used and its derivation (effective number of tests). (5) A statement of the structure correction applied (number of PCs, mixed model + kinship source) and the relatedness-pruning step. Justification: a peak is only interpretable relative to a calibrated null and a stated multiple-testing threshold, and the correction/pruning provenance is what makes lambda ≈ 1 credible rather than accidental.

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap:** "A tall, genome-wide-significant peak is a real finding on its own." → **surfacing question:** I'll show you a beautiful Manhattan peak but hide the QQ plot and lambda. Tell me whether you can trust the peak yet, and exactly what you need to see first and why.
- **Trap:** "Inflation (lambda > 1) means lots of true polygenic signal — good news." → **surfacing question:** Your uncorrected scan has lambda 1.6 and almost *every* marker looks mildly significant. Distinguish 'the whole genome affects this trait' from 'structure is leaking into every test' — which is it, and how would you tell them apart?
- **Trap:** "PC covariates fully handle relatedness, so the mixed model is redundant." → **surfacing question:** You corrected with 10 PCs but left two sib-pairs in. Will the PCs absorb the sibs' shared genetics? If not, what residual problem remains and which method fixes it?
- **Trap:** "Lower lambda is always better, so add as many PCs as possible." → **surfacing question:** After 30 PCs your lambda is 0.85. Is that an improvement over 1.0? What might you have done to your real signal?
- **Trap:** "Per-marker p < 0.05 is the significance bar, same as any other test." → **surfacing question:** You test 500,000 markers at p < 0.05. How many false positives do you expect from chance alone, and what does that force you to do to the threshold?
- **Trap:** "GWAS jointly models all markers to find the causal one." → **surfacing question:** Is a genome-wide scan one big regression or many small ones? Given your answer, when one causal variant exists, why does the Manhattan plot show a *peak* (many markers) rather than a single spike?

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar context (a crop-breeding scenario).
- **Q:** A rice breeding team runs a GWAS for grain yield on 800 accessions drawn from the two major subspecies, *indica* and *japonica*, which differ both in average yield *and* in allele frequency across huge stretches of the genome. They run a naive scan, get a gorgeous Manhattan plot with dozens of peaks and lambda = 2.1, and excitedly start designing markers for marker-assisted selection on those peaks. (a) Explain precisely why this scan is almost certainly a structure artifact rather than a yield-gene discovery, naming the confounding path. (b) What does the QQ plot for this scan look like and what does lambda 2.1 tell you? (c) The team also included several pairs of full-sib lines from the same crosses — explain why PC covariates for the indica/japonica split alone will not fully fix the scan and what does. (d) What would convince you a surviving peak is a real yield locus worth putting into MAS?
  *Answer sketch:* (a) *indica* and *japonica* differ in mean yield and in genome-wide allele frequency, so any marker whose frequency tracks subspecies correlates with yield through subspecies, not causation — the confounder is subspecies ancestry, and because frequency differences span large regions, dozens of regions light up at once. (b) The QQ bulk lifts massively off the diagonal (almost all markers look significant); lambda 2.1 means severe genomic inflation — the structure correction failed, so *no* peak is trustworthy yet. (c) PCs for the two-subspecies split capture broad ancestry but not the fine pairwise relatedness of full-sib lines sharing large segments; that residual relatedness keeps inflating the scan. A kinship-based mixed model (random effect from the genomic relationship matrix), ideally combined with the subspecies PCs as fixed covariates, absorbs both the subspecies axis and the sib relatedness. (d) A real yield locus should: survive structure + relatedness correction with lambda driven to ≈ 1 and a QQ bulk back on the diagonal, clear the properly-derived genome-wide threshold (Bonferroni for the effective number of independent markers, not p < 0.05), replicate in an independent panel or cross, and ideally make biological sense / fine-map to a plausible candidate. The transfer is recognizing the indica/japonica divide as a textbook structure confounder, the sib-lines as the relatedness problem only the mixed model fixes, and the QQ plot + lambda as the honesty check that must pass *before* any peak earns a place in marker-assisted selection.
