# p4m3 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p4m3-deep-learning-on-genomic-data|p4m3 · Deep Learning on Genomic Data]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Three claims sit at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to one of them: **(A)** deep nets shine in genomics when *raw sequence or spatial structure* carries signal a linear marker model discards — regulatory grammar, motifs, splicing — because CNN/attention architectures are built to extract exactly that structure; **(B)** on *tabular genotype matrices* deep nets frequently fail to beat GBLUP, because there is little non-linear signal to exploit, the data are additive and high-dimensional (which a shrinkage linear model already represents exactly), and a 0/1/2 marker table offers neither image-like nor sequence-like structure for convolution or attention to grab; and **(C)** deep learning's advantage depends on sample size — with n small relative to the number of markers, a high-capacity net overfits and generalizes worse than a shrunk linear model.

Grounded references: the load-bearing read is Abdollahi-Arpanahi, Gianola & Peñagaricano (2020), *Genetics Selection Evolution* 52:12 — a head-to-head of MLP, CNN, random forest, gradient boosting, GBLUP, and Bayes B. On the real bull/Holstein dataset the best predictive correlation came from gradient boosting (0.36), then Bayes B (0.34) and GBLUP (0.33), with CNN (0.29) and MLP (0.26) *behind* the classical methods; deep learning did relatively better only under simulated non-additive gene action. Reviews: Eraslan, Avsec, Gagneur & Theis (2019), *Nature Reviews Genetics* 20:389–403 (where DL genuinely shines: raw sequence — regulatory grammar, splicing, chromatin); Zou, Huss, Abid et al. (2019), *Nature Genetics* 51:12–18 (primer + honest data-requirement caveats). All confirmed.

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** What is the inductive bias of a convolutional neural network, and what kind of input is it built to exploit?
   *Answer sketch:* A CNN assumes *local spatial structure with translation invariance* — that a meaningful pattern (a motif/feature) can appear at different positions and should be detected the same way (weight sharing across a sliding filter/kernel). It is built for inputs where adjacency means something: images, and DNA *sequence* (motifs that recur along the strand). Watch for "CNN = just a deep net"; the point is the spatial/locality bias.
2. **Q:** What does a transformer's attention mechanism do, and what input structure does it assume is informative?
   *Answer sketch:* Attention lets every position weight and aggregate information from every other position, learning which long-range relationships matter — it assumes the input is a *sequence with meaningful dependencies* between elements (language, or genomic sequence with long-range regulatory interactions). Its power comes from modeling context across a structured sequence.
3. **Q:** What does "tabular data" mean for a genotype matrix, and how does it differ from "raw sequence"?
   *Answer sketch:* Tabular = rows are individuals, columns are markers; each cell is an allele dosage (0/1/2). The columns have no inherent image-like neighborhood or sequential grammar that the model can exploit — reordering columns does not destroy meaning the way scrambling pixels or letters would. Raw sequence is the actual ordered string of bases, where adjacency and order carry biological grammar (motifs, reading frame, splice sites). (Claim B.)
4. **Q:** From Abdollahi-Arpanahi et al. (2020), recall the real-data ordering of methods by predictive correlation. Which methods led, and where did the deep nets land?
   *Answer sketch:* On the real dataset: gradient boosting ≈ 0.36 (best), then Bayes B ≈ 0.34 and GBLUP ≈ 0.33, with CNN ≈ 0.29 and MLP ≈ 0.26 *behind* the classical methods. The deep nets lost to the classical/parametric methods on real, mostly-additive data. (Claim B.)

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** An MLP is a universal function approximator — it can in principle represent any function. Why doesn't that universality translate into beating GBLUP on a tabular genotype matrix?
   *Answer sketch:* Universal approximation is about *representational capacity given enough data*, not about *generalizing from limited data*. On a small, additive, high-dimensional genotype matrix the function to learn is essentially a linear sum of small effects — which GBLUP already represents exactly. The net's extra capacity is spent approximating a function that does not generalize (fitting noise), so capacity becomes a liability, not an asset. (Claims B, C.)
2. **Q:** Deep learning's famous wins (vision, language) came from CNNs and transformers. What property of those inputs powered the wins, and what does a 0/1/2 marker table fail to offer those architectures?
   *Answer sketch:* The wins came from architectures whose inductive biases match real structure in the input — spatial locality/translation invariance in images, sequential/long-range grammar in language. A tabular marker matrix has neither: columns are not spatially organized in a way convolution can exploit, and there is no positional grammar across the row for attention to model. So the inductive biases that make those architectures powerful elsewhere give *no* advantage here. (Claim B.)
3. **Q:** The trait is mostly additive and a linear model can represent a sum of additive marker effects exactly. What is the deep net's non-linear machinery adding here — and why can "adding nothing useful" still cost you?
   *Answer sketch:* It is adding the ability to model interactions/non-linearities that, on an additive trait, mostly are not present. Because the useful signal is already captured linearly, the non-linear machinery has nothing real to find — but it still has the *freedom* to fit spurious patterns in finite data, so with limited n it overfits and generalizes worse. Useless flexibility is not neutral; it costs out-of-sample accuracy. (Claims B, C.)
4. **Q:** State the relationship between sample size, parameter count, and a deep net's ability to generalize, and apply it to ~600 individuals and ~1,300 markers.
   *Answer sketch:* Generalization requires the data (n examples) to constrain the model's parameters; deep nets have very many parameters and need correspondingly large n (DL's wins came with thousands to millions of examples). With ~600 individuals and ~1,300 markers, parameters can vastly outnumber examples, so the net is under-constrained, drives training loss low while held-out accuracy stays mediocre — classic overfitting. A shrunk linear model, with far fewer effective degrees of freedom, generalizes better here. (Claim C.)

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** You train an MLP on the wheat marker matrix and watch the curves: training loss falls toward zero while held-out predictive ability plateaus well below GBLUP's. Name the pattern and the two levers you would pull first.
   *Answer sketch:* The pattern is overfitting (memorizing training, failing to generalize). First levers: stronger regularization (dropout, weight decay, smaller network) and reducing capacity (fewer layers/units) — and, per Azodi et al., feature selection before the net when markers vastly outnumber lines. Early stopping on a validation split also helps. The deeper lesson: on this data the ceiling is set by additive signal + small n, so these levers narrow the gap but rarely overturn GBLUP. (Claim C, Claim B.)
2. **Q:** You add a 1-D CNN that slides over markers in genome order, reasoning that adjacent markers are in LD. Predict whether this helps, and why or why not.
   *Answer sketch:* It rarely helps much. The CNN's locality bias assumes *meaningful* local spatial structure; LD between adjacent markers is real but it is mostly *redundancy* (correlated tags of the same region), not the kind of compositional motif structure convolution was built to extract from raw sequence. There is no positional "grammar" along a marker panel the way there is along actual bases. So the convolution buys little over a model that already uses all markers' relatedness (GBLUP). It would matter far more on *raw sequence*. (Claim B, contrast with Claim A.)
3. **Q:** Add your best deep net as one more row in the p4m2 benchmark table, run through the identical CV folds. You get MLP 0.26 and CNN 0.29 against GBLUP 0.33, with ~0.04 fold-to-fold spread. Write the honest verdict.
   *Answer sketch:* The deep nets did *not* beat GBLUP; they sit below it, and given the ~0.04 spread the gap is real-to-marginal but certainly not in the nets' favor. Verdict: on this tabular, additive, small-n dataset, deep learning fails to beat the classical baseline — qualitatively reproducing Abdollahi-Arpanahi et al.'s real-data ordering (GB > Bayes B > GBLUP > CNN > MLP). Do not reach for DL by default here. (Claim B; grounded in the 2020 paper.)
4. **Q:** You sweep network capacity (more layers/units) on this ~600-line dataset and measure held-out predictive ability at each size. Predict the shape of the curve and explain it.
   *Answer sketch:* Held-out predictive ability does *not* rise monotonically with capacity; it typically flattens then *declines* as capacity grows, because more parameters on fixed small n increase overfitting. Bigger hurts out-of-sample here. This directly demonstrates the sample-size-vs-parameter-count claim (Claim C): capacity helps only when data can constrain it.

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Abdollahi-Arpanahi et al. (2020) found deep nets lost on real data but did *relatively better* under simulated non-additive gene action. What does the contrast isolate, and why is the simulation the cleaner test?
   *Answer sketch:* The contrast isolates *genetic architecture* as the variable that decides whether DL helps. On real (mostly additive) data the nets had no non-linear signal to exploit and lost; under simulated strong non-additivity there was genuine interaction structure for their non-linear machinery to capture, so they closed or reversed the gap. The simulation is cleaner because the true architecture is *known and controlled* — you can dial non-additivity up and watch the methods respond, removing the confound of unknown real-world architecture. (Claims A/B; the conditions that flip the result.)
2. **Q:** "Deep learning is the most powerful method, so it should win at genomic prediction." Take the claim apart: where does the power actually come from, and why doesn't it transfer to a marker table?
   *Answer sketch:* DL's power comes from *exploiting structure in the input* via matched inductive biases — convolution exploits spatial locality, attention exploits sequence context. That power transfers only when such structure exists in the input. A tabular, additive genotype matrix contains neither spatial nor sequential grammar and little non-linear signal, so there is nothing for the powerful machinery to grab; "powerful in general" ≠ "powerful here." (Claim B; Claim A's converse.)
3. **Q:** Distinguish what changes for a deep net if the input were *raw DNA sequence around a regulatory region* instead of a 0/1/2 marker matrix. Which obstacles relax?
   *Answer sketch:* Raw sequence has the spatial/compositional structure CNNs are built for — motifs, binding-site grammar, splice signals — that a fixed marker table discards entirely. So the "no exploitable structure" obstacle (Claim B) relaxes: convolutions can learn motifs a linear model over fixed marker features cannot represent (this is exactly the regime Eraslan et al. document as DL's genuine genomics win, Claim A). The small-n obstacle (Claim C) may persist unless data are abundant, but the *structure* obstacle is the one that flips.
4. **Q:** Why is feature selection (per Azodi et al.) especially important for neural nets when markers vastly outnumber lines, and what failure does it mitigate?
   *Answer sketch:* When markers ≫ lines, a dense net's first layer alone has enormous parameter count tied to noisy, mostly-uninformative inputs, worsening overfitting (Claim C). Selecting informative features first reduces input dimensionality and the parameter burden, giving the limited training data a fighting chance to constrain the net. It mitigates the overfit-on-noise failure that sinks naive deep nets on high-dimensional, small-n genomic data.

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A breeding outfit is considering investing a year of engineering effort into a deep-learning genomic-prediction platform. Given everything in this module, advise them: when is the investment justified, and when is it not? Make the call.
   *Answer sketch:* Not justified for routine prediction on tabular SNP panels of mostly-additive traits with modest n — GBLUP matches or beats DL there at a fraction of the cost and complexity (Claim B). The investment becomes defensible when (i) the inputs are *raw sequence* and the goal is regulatory/functional signal (Claim A), (ii) traits have strong, real non-additive architecture (epistasis/G×E) confirmed to be learnable, and/or (iii) training data are large relative to features (Claim C). Recommend piloting DL only against a strong GBLUP baseline on the identical CV folds and adopting it only if it wins by more than fold-to-fold noise.
2. **Q:** Design an experiment that demonstrates the sample-size-dependence of deep learning's advantage in genomic prediction, controlling architecture.
   *Answer sketch:* Hold the trait architecture fixed (or simulate a known non-additive architecture where DL *can* help). Subsample the training population at increasing sizes (e.g., 200, 500, 1000, 2000, …, up to many thousands), and at each size run GBLUP and a deep net through identical held-out CV. Plot held-out predictive ability vs. n for both. Confirming result: at small n the net trails GBLUP (overfits); as n grows the net's curve rises faster and eventually crosses GBLUP (capacity finally constrained by data) — directly evidencing Claim C. If the architecture is purely additive, the net should never durably overtake GBLUP, separating the sample-size effect from the architecture effect.

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: deep learning is the most powerful method, so it should win at genomic prediction.** → **surfacing question:** "On a real tabular SNP dataset of a few hundred lines, an MLP and CNN both land *below* GBLUP. If DL is so powerful, why did it lose?" (Correct: DL's power is exploiting input structure; a tabular additive matrix has none for it to grab, and small n makes its capacity overfit. Claim B.)
- **Trap: universal approximation means a net can always learn the best predictor given enough layers.** → **surfacing question:** "Universal approximation says the net *can represent* the function. What does it say about *generalizing* from 600 examples — and why does that distinction sink the net here?" (Correct: representation ≠ generalization; with tiny n the net fits noise. Claim C.)
- **Trap: a 1-D CNN over markers exploits LD, so it should beat a plain marker model.** → **surfacing question:** "LD between adjacent markers is redundancy, not motif grammar. What is a convolution built to extract, and does a marker panel offer it?" (Correct: convolution extracts compositional/spatial motifs from raw sequence; a marker table's adjacency is mostly correlated redundancy, not grammar, so the CNN gains little. Claim B; contrast Claim A.)
- **Trap: deep learning never helps in genomics.** → **surfacing question:** "Name the input and trait conditions under which a deep net genuinely *beats* a linear marker model in genomics, and why each helps." (Correct: raw sequence with regulatory grammar/motifs/splicing, strong real non-additivity, and large n — DL wins where it can exploit structure or interactions the linear model discards. Claim A, Claim C.)
- **Trap: a low training loss means the deep net learned the genetics well.** → **surfacing question:** "Your net's training loss is near zero but held-out predictive ability is below GBLUP. What is that pattern called, and what did the net actually 'learn'?" (Correct: overfitting — it memorized training-sample noise, not generalizable genetic signal. Claim C.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A tomato program has two prediction problems and a budget to build *one* deep-learning capability. Problem 1: predict fruit yield (a classic polygenic, largely additive trait) for ~500 inbred lines genotyped on a 5,000-SNP panel — a tabular genotype matrix. Problem 2: predict, from the *raw promoter sequence* of a candidate gene, whether an allele up- or down-regulates a fruit-ripening transcription factor, using thousands of labeled promoter variants where the regulatory motif arrangement matters. A vendor pitches the same deep architecture for both. Answer: (1) For Problem 1, predict whether a deep net will beat GBLUP and justify it via genetic architecture, lack of exploitable structure, and the line-to-marker ratio — and name the real-data study whose ordering you expect to reproduce. (2) For Problem 2, explain why a deep net (specifically a CNN or attention-based model) is the *right* tool, naming the precise structure in the input it exploits that a linear marker model would discard. (3) State the general principle that explains why the same architecture is right for one problem and wrong for the other, and use it to tell the program where to spend its single budget.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) For Problem 1 the deep net will most likely *not* beat GBLUP. The trait is mostly additive, so a linear shrinkage model represents the sum of small marker effects exactly; the tabular SNP matrix offers no spatial or sequential structure for convolution or attention to exploit; and with only ~500 lines against 5,000 markers the net is badly under-constrained and overfits (Claims B, C). This qualitatively reproduces Abdollahi-Arpanahi, Gianola & Peñagaricano (2020), whose real-data ordering put gradient boosting (0.36), Bayes B (0.34), and GBLUP (0.33) *ahead* of CNN (0.29) and MLP (0.26). (2) For Problem 2 a CNN or attention model is the right tool: the input is *raw promoter sequence*, whose value lies in the local arrangement and composition of regulatory motifs (transcription-factor binding sites) and their context — exactly the spatial/sequential grammar convolution and attention are built to extract, and exactly the signal a linear model over fixed marker features would discard (Claim A). The thousands of labeled variants also supply the sample size deep nets need (Claim C). (3) The general principle: deep learning's advantage comes from *exploiting structure (spatial/sequential) or genuine non-linearity in the input* that a linear model cannot represent — present in raw sequence, absent in a tabular additive genotype matrix. Therefore the program should spend its single deep-learning budget on Problem 2 (the raw-sequence regulatory problem) and keep GBLUP for Problem 1's tabular genomic prediction.
