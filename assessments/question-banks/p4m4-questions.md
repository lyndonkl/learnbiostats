# p4m4 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p4m4-reading-agricultural-ml-papers|p4m4 · Reading Agricultural ML Papers]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Two claims sit at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to one of them: **(A)** a genomic-prediction paper is judged on its cross-validation design and its baseline — a method that beats nothing, or that leaks relatedness across folds, reports inflated accuracy regardless of how sophisticated it sounds; and **(B)** the right baseline to demand is GBLUP, and a fair cross-validation must hold out the *unit the method claims to predict* (new lines, new families, or new environments), because using a looser split inflates the number.

Grounded references / exemplars: Azodi et al. (2019), *G3* 9(11):3691–3702 — an honest multi-method, multi-species benchmark with consistent CV, used here as the template a good paper should resemble. Crossa et al. (2017), *Trends in Plant Science* 22(11):961–975 — a map of the field's methods and prediction scenarios. Ferrão, Amadeu, Benevenuto, de Bem Oliveira & Munoz (2021), *Frontiers in Plant Science* 12:676326 — genomic selection in an outcrossing autotetraploid (blueberry), where ploidy and outcrossing complicate relatedness and the realistic prediction target. CV-scenario vocabulary: CV1 (predict lines with no phenotype in any environment), CV2 (predict lines partially observed in some environments), CV0 (predict untested environments), plus leave-family-out and leave-environment-out. Relationship: accuracy = predictive ability / sqrt(h²). All citations confirmed.

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** Name the two things in a genomic-prediction paper's *methods* section you check first, before the headline accuracy.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) The cross-validation design — how the folds were made (random lines? whole families held out? across environments? across years?). (2) The baseline — what the proposed method was compared against (GBLUP? nothing? a hobbled version of itself?). (Claim A — both live in the methods, not the abstract.)
2. **Q:** What is relatedness leakage across CV folds?
   *Answer sketch:* When related individuals (e.g., full sibs, or clones/replicates of the same line) are split between the training and test folds, so the model can "predict" a test individual partly by recognizing its family/genotype already seen in training — inflating accuracy without the method being better at predicting genuinely new genotypes. (Claim A.)
3. **Q:** Define predictive ability and accuracy as used in this literature, and state the relationship between them.
   *Answer sketch:* Predictive ability = correlation between predicted and *observed phenotype* in held-out data. Accuracy = correlation between predicted and *true breeding value*; commonly estimated as accuracy = predictive ability / sqrt(h²). So a paper reporting "accuracy" has divided by a heritability estimate; checking which quantity is reported (and which h²) matters when comparing across papers.
4. **Q:** What do CV1, CV2, and CV0 refer to in plant-breeding genomic prediction?
   *Answer sketch:* CV1 = predicting lines that have *no* phenotypic records in any environment (newly developed lines). CV2 = predicting lines that have *some* records in some environments but are missing in others (sparse testing / incomplete field design). CV0 = predicting performance in *untested environments*. They differ in how hard the prediction task is and in what is held out. (Claim B — the scenario must match the claim.)

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** Why does relatedness leakage inflate accuracy without the method actually being better? Connect it to p4m1's claim that genomic prediction *is* a relatedness model.
   *Answer sketch:* Genomic prediction works by borrowing across realized relatedness (p4m1, Claim A there). If a line's relatives sit in the training fold while the line is in the test fold, the model predicts it largely by recognizing the family — exploiting the very relatedness mechanism that makes prediction work, but in a way that will *not* generalize to a genuinely new, unrelated family. The high number measures "predict a relative of training lines," not "predict a new line," so it overstates real-world performance. (Claim A, the leakage core.)
2. **Q:** A paper's proposed method "beats no prediction" / beats only a deliberately weak comparison. Why does that tell you almost nothing, and why is GBLUP the baseline to demand?
   *Answer sketch:* Beating "no prediction" (the mean) or a strawman is trivial — almost any genomic model clears that bar, so it provides no evidence the new method is *worth using*. GBLUP is the right baseline because it is the cheap, well-understood, hard-to-beat classical standard (p4m1–p4m3 showed it routinely matches or beats fancier methods on additive traits). A method that does not beat GBLUP has shown no value; a paper omitting GBLUP has not made its case. (Claim A, Claim B.)
3. **Q:** Why must a fair cross-validation hold out the *unit the method claims to predict*? Give the consequence of a mismatch.
   *Answer sketch:* The CV must simulate the real deployment task. If you claim to predict *new families* but split folds by random line (sibs spread across folds), the test estimate reflects within-family prediction (easy) not new-family prediction (hard) — inflating the number for the claimed use case. Likewise claiming to predict *new environments* requires leave-environment-out; a within-environment random split overstates it. Mismatch = the reported accuracy answers an easier question than the one the paper claims to answer. (Claim B.)
4. **Q:** Wheat, maize, and blueberry are bred very differently — inbred lines, hybrids, and an outcrossing autotetraploid. How does breeding structure change what a fair CV should hold out and what "predicting a new individual" means?
   *Answer sketch:* In inbred wheat, a "line" is a near-homozygous genotype reproducible across plots; predicting a new line = a new genotype. In hybrid maize, the unit may be a *cross* (parental combination), and you may need to hold out parents/testers to predict untested hybrids. In outcrossing autotetraploid blueberry (Ferrão et al. 2021), each individual is highly heterozygous and unique, ploidy complicates the relationship matrix, and families segregate widely — so a fair CV must hold out whole families/crosses to mean "predict a new seedling," and relatedness is harder to define. The right hold-out unit is crop-specific. (Claim B.)

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** A paper reports 0.85 predictive ability for a deep-learning method on a maize dataset using "10-fold random cross-validation," and no GBLUP baseline. Walk through your read: what do you check, what do you suspect, and what would you ask the authors?
   *Answer sketch:* Check the CV design: random 10-fold on individual rows likely splits siblings/relatives (and possibly replicate plots of the same line) across train and test → relatedness leakage, inflating 0.85 (Claim A). Check the baseline: none reported, so even if 0.85 were real you cannot tell it beats GBLUP — possibly GBLUP also gets ~0.85 cheaply (Claim B). Suspect: leaky CV + missing baseline = the result may be empty. Ask: (i) were related individuals/replicates kept within the same fold (leave-family-out)? (ii) what does GBLUP score on the identical folds? (iii) is 0.85 predictive ability or accuracy, and against what h²?
2. **Q:** Build the structured summary you would fill in for any genomic-prediction paper. What fields, and which one carries your verdict?
   *Answer sketch:* Fields: trait; species; dataset (n lines, n markers, n environments); CV design (random line / leave-family-out / leave-environment-out / CV1-2-0); baseline (GBLUP? Bayes? none?); metric (predictive ability / accuracy / RMSE) and h² if accuracy; the result (numbers per method); and a one-line verdict on whether the CV and baseline make the result *trustworthy*. The verdict field is the point — a paper can have a high number and a "not trustworthy" verdict if the CV leaks or the baseline is missing. (Claim A.)
3. **Q:** A paper reports both a random-line CV and a stricter leave-family-out CV for the same method. Random-line accuracy is 0.62; leave-family-out is 0.38. Interpret the gap.
   *Answer sketch:* The drop from 0.62 to 0.38 is the relatedness-leakage premium: the random-line number was inflated by sibs shared across folds; the leave-family-out number reflects genuine new-family prediction and is the honest estimate for that deployment scenario. The 0.62 is not "wrong" for predicting within known families, but quoting it as the method's general accuracy would mislead. Praise the paper for reporting both. (Claims A, B.)
4. **Q:** Using the PubMed/arXiv search habit from this module, you pull a wheat paper and a blueberry paper. Both report ~0.5 accuracy. Why might those two numbers *not* be comparable even at face value?
   *Answer sketch:* Different crops, traits, heritabilities, marker densities, training sizes, *and* CV scenarios. Accuracy = predictive ability / sqrt(h²), so the same predictive ability divided by a different h² gives a different "accuracy." Blueberry's autotetraploid outcrossing structure (Ferrão et al. 2021) makes relatedness and the prediction target differ from inbred wheat, and the CV hold-out unit differs. Comparable numbers require comparable CV strictness, metric definition, and prediction scenario — which they likely lack. (Claim B; the metric definition.)

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Across many agricultural ML papers, what is the single most common way an inflated accuracy gets reported? Name the pattern so it can be spotted instantly.
   *Answer sketch:* A lenient (random-line) cross-validation that lets related individuals (or replicate plots of the same line) fall on both sides of the split — relatedness leakage — often paired with a missing or weak baseline. The headline accuracy then measures "predict relatives of the training set," not "predict new genotypes," and there is nothing strong to compare it against. Spotting it: go straight to the fold-construction sentence and the baseline list. (Claim A.)
2. **Q:** "This method is sophisticated and novel, so the high accuracy is impressive." Take the claim apart using this module's standard.
   *Answer sketch:* Sophistication is irrelevant to whether the *number means anything*. The number's validity rests entirely on (i) whether the CV held out the claimed prediction unit without relatedness leakage and (ii) whether the method beat a real GBLUP baseline. A sophisticated method with a leaky CV or no baseline reports an empty result; a plain method with a clean leave-family-out CV beating GBLUP reports a real one. Judge the design, not the adjectives. (Claim A, the keystone.)
3. **Q:** Two papers each beat GBLUP. Paper X beats it by 0.01 in a single random-line CV with no error bars; Paper Y beats it by 0.04 averaged over repeated leave-family-out CV with fold-to-fold spread reported. Which claim do you believe more, and why?
   *Answer sketch:* Paper Y. Its margin (0.04) exceeds plausible noise and is reported with spread, under a strict scenario that does not leak relatedness — so the improvement is both credible and meaningful for predicting new families. Paper X's 0.01 in a single lenient split with no spread is within noise and possibly leakage-inflated for both methods; the "win" is not distinguishable from chance. Believe the result whose *design* makes the margin interpretable. (Claims A, B; ties to the noise discipline from p4m2/p4m3.)
4. **Q:** Why is leave-environment-out CV the honest test when a paper claims to predict performance in *new environments*, and what does within-environment random splitting hide?
   *Answer sketch:* Predicting a new environment (CV0) requires the model to extrapolate to environmental conditions it never saw — so the environment must be held out entirely. Within-environment random splitting lets the model learn each environment's mean/effects from the very environment it is "predicting," hiding the G×E extrapolation difficulty and inflating accuracy. The looser split answers "predict a new line in a *known* environment," not "predict a *new* environment" — a different, easier question. (Claim B; ties to p5 G×E.)

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** You are reviewing a manuscript claiming a transformer beats GBLUP for genomic prediction in chickpea. What must the paper contain for you to recommend acceptance, and what single omission would make you reject regardless of the accuracy?
   *Answer sketch:* For acceptance: (i) a GBLUP baseline run on the *identical* folds; (ii) a CV design that holds out the claimed prediction unit (e.g., leave-family-out if claiming new-line prediction) with no relatedness leakage and no replicate-plot leakage; (iii) repeated CV with reported fold-to-fold spread so the margin over GBLUP can be judged against noise; (iv) clear statement of metric (predictive ability vs accuracy, and h²). Automatic reject regardless of the number: no GBLUP baseline (or only a strawman) — without it the paper has not shown the transformer is worth anything (Claim B). A leaky CV would be the second fatal flaw (Claim A).
2. **Q:** Apply this module's standard to *your own* benchmark. Design the audit you would run on it before publishing, and state the two conclusions that would force you to revise it.
   *Answer sketch:* Audit: (1) Leakage check — confirm no line's relatives or replicate records straddle train/test folds; if the deployment target is new families/environments, rebuild folds as leave-family-out / leave-environment-out and re-run every method through the identical folds. (2) Baseline check — confirm a real GBLUP (not a hobbled one) is in the table, run on the same folds. (3) Noise check — repeat the CV and report spread so any method's margin is judged against fold-to-fold variation. Forced revision if: (a) relatedness leakage is found (accuracies must be recomputed under a stricter split, expecting them to drop); or (b) the GBLUP baseline is missing or weak (must be added, and any "win" re-evaluated against it). This holds your work to the bar you hold the literature to. (Claims A, B.)

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: a sophisticated/novel method with a high accuracy is a strong result.** → **surfacing question:** "Before believing the headline number, what two things in the methods must you confirm — and what do you conclude if the CV leaks relatedness or there's no GBLUP baseline?" (Correct: confirm CV design and baseline; if either fails, the result is empty however sophisticated the method. Claim A.)
- **Trap: random K-fold cross-validation is the rigorous, default-correct choice.** → **surfacing question:** "If full sibs (or replicate plots of the same line) are randomly split across folds, what is the model really being tested on, and is that the task the paper claims?" (Correct: it tests prediction of relatives already represented in training — relatedness leakage — not new genotypes; the CV must hold out the claimed unit. Claims A, B.)
- **Trap: beating 'no prediction' or a simple model proves the method works.** → **surfacing question:** "The method beats the population mean by a lot. Does that show it's worth using over GBLUP, and what baseline would actually settle it?" (Correct: beating the mean is trivial; only beating a real GBLUP baseline on the same folds shows value. Claim B.)
- **Trap: predictive ability and accuracy are the same number, so papers are directly comparable.** → **surfacing question:** "One paper reports predictive ability 0.50, another reports accuracy 0.50. Given accuracy = predictive ability / sqrt(h²), are these the same, and can you compare them directly?" (Correct: no — accuracy is predictive ability divided by sqrt(h²), so the underlying predictive abilities differ; comparing requires matching metric, h², and CV scenario.)
- **Trap: a stricter CV (leave-family/environment-out) is just being pessimistic; the random-CV number is the 'true' accuracy.** → **surfacing question:** "If the program's real goal is to predict brand-new families, which CV number — random-line or leave-family-out — is the relevant 'true' accuracy, and why is the higher one misleading here?" (Correct: leave-family-out is the relevant estimate for that goal; the higher random-line number is inflated by leakage and answers an easier question. Claim B.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A cassava breeding paper claims a graph-neural-network method achieves 0.78 predictive ability for fresh-root yield, "far exceeding traditional methods." Cassava is clonally propagated and outcrossing, the dataset comprises several large full-sib families plus clonal replicates of each genotype across trials, and the stated goal is to predict the merit of *newly generated seedlings from new crosses* before field testing. The methods section says: "genotypes were randomly assigned to 5 folds; the GNN was compared to a single-hidden-layer neural network." Answer: (1) Identify the two most likely ways this 0.78 is inflated, naming the exact mechanism for each and tying one of them back to *why* genomic prediction works at all. (2) Explain why the chosen comparison method fails as a baseline and name the baseline the paper should have used. (3) Specify how the cross-validation should have been built to match the stated prediction goal in this clonal, outcrossing crop, and predict the direction the reported 0.78 would move under that stricter design. (4) Give your one-line verdict on whether the headline claim "far exceeding traditional methods" is supported, and state the general principle by which you judged it.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) Two inflation mechanisms. First, *relatedness leakage*: with several large full-sib families, random fold assignment scatters sibs across train and test, so the GNN predicts test individuals by recognizing their families — exploiting the realized-relatedness mechanism that makes genomic prediction work (p4m1) but in a way that will not generalize to seedlings from *new* crosses; this directly inflates 0.78 (Claim A). Second, *replicate/clone leakage*: clonal replicates of the *same genotype* sit in multiple trials, so the identical genotype can appear in both train and test folds — the model effectively "predicts" genotypes it has already seen, an even more direct leak. (2) The comparison method (a single-hidden-layer neural network) is not a recognized strong baseline; beating it shows nothing about whether the GNN beats the field standard. The paper should have used a real GBLUP baseline run on the identical folds (Claim B). (3) To match the goal of predicting seedlings from new crosses in a clonal, outcrossing crop, the CV must hold out *whole families/crosses* (leave-family-out), and all clonal replicates of any genotype must be confined to a single fold so no genotype straddles train and test; ideally repeat the CV and report fold-to-fold spread. Under that stricter design the reported 0.78 would be expected to *drop*, plausibly substantially, toward the honest new-family prediction level. (4) Verdict: the "far exceeding traditional methods" claim is *not* supported — it rests on a leaky random CV and a strawman baseline rather than a clean leave-family-out comparison against GBLUP. The general principle: a genomic-prediction result means something only when its cross-validation holds out the unit it claims to predict (no relatedness or replicate leakage) and it beats a real GBLUP baseline; sophistication of the method is irrelevant to that judgment (Claims A, B).
