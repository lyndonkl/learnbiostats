# p4m2 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p4m2-ml-approaches-ridge-elastic-net-rf-gradient-boosting|p4m2 · ML Approaches: Ridge, Elastic Net, RF, Gradient Boosting]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

Three claims sit at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to one of them: **(A)** for mostly-additive traits, penalized linear models usually match or beat tree ensembles, because the genetic architecture is additive and high-dimensional — exactly what shrinkage was designed for and what trees waste capacity fighting; **(B)** ML earns its keep mainly where non-additive effects or G×E matter, and absent those, added flexibility does not improve out-of-sample prediction; and **(C)** the honest metric is cross-validated predictive ability (held-out predicted-vs-observed correlation), not training fit, which any flexible model can inflate by memorizing.

Grounded references: the load-bearing benchmark is Azodi et al. (2019), *G3* 9(11):3691–3702 — six linear and six non-linear algorithms across 18 traits in six plant species; the headline is that linear/parametric methods were competitive with or better than ML across most traits, with the ranking depending on genetic architecture and training-set size, and that feature selection was critical for neural nets when markers greatly outnumbered training lines. Software: scikit-learn (`Ridge`, `Lasso`, `ElasticNetCV`, `RandomForestRegressor`, `GradientBoostingRegressor`, `KFold`, `cross_val_score`); XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin 2016, KDD '16:785–794). All confirmed.

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** Name the penalty each of ridge, LASSO, and elastic net imposes, and what each does to coefficients.
   *Answer sketch:* Ridge = L2 penalty (sum of squared coefficients) → shrinks all coefficients toward zero but keeps them all non-zero. LASSO = L1 penalty (sum of absolute values) → shrinks and sets many coefficients exactly to zero (selection). Elastic net = a weighted blend of L1 and L2 → both shrinks and selects. Watch for swapping L1/L2.
2. **Q:** In one sentence each, what is a random forest doing and what is gradient boosting doing?
   *Answer sketch:* Random forest = bagging — many deep, de-correlated trees grown on bootstrap samples and random feature subsets, then averaged (variance reduction). Gradient boosting = additive stage-wise trees, each fit to the residual errors of the current ensemble, with a learning rate (bias reduction by sequential correction). Both are built to capture interactions/non-linearities.
3. **Q:** Define "predictive ability" as used in this module, and how it is computed in a K-fold scheme.
   *Answer sketch:* Predictive ability = the Pearson correlation between predicted and observed phenotypes in *held-out* folds. In K-fold CV: split lines into K folds; for each fold, train on the other K−1 folds, predict the held-out fold, correlate predicted with observed; report the mean (and spread) across folds. (Claim C — it is the held-out, not training, correlation.)
4. **Q:** What does "training fit" measure, and why is it not the metric of interest here?
   *Answer sketch:* Training fit measures how well the model reproduces the data it was *fit on* (e.g., training-set correlation or R²). It is not the metric because any sufficiently flexible model can drive it arbitrarily high by memorizing noise; it says nothing about prediction on unseen individuals. (Claim C.)

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** The genetic architecture you studied in Phase 2 is overwhelmingly additive — many small effects summing. Tree ensembles are machines for finding interactions and non-linearities. If the true signal is additive, what is a tree ensemble spending its capacity on, and why might that hurt it?
   *Answer sketch:* It spends capacity searching for interaction/threshold structure that largely is not there, fitting spurious non-linear patterns in the training sample (noise) instead of the additive signal. A linear model can represent a sum of additive effects *exactly* and devotes all its (regularized) capacity to estimating them; the ensemble's flexibility is wasted and can actively hurt by overfitting, especially with limited n. (Claim A, Claim B.)
2. **Q:** Random forest and gradient boosting almost always fit the training data better than ridge — yet on held-out folds the linear model often matches or beats them. What does that gap reveal?
   *Answer sketch:* It reveals the difference between learning a generalizable pattern and memorizing the training set. High training fit + lower held-out correlation = overfitting; the flexible model captured sample-specific noise that does not recur out of sample. This is precisely why training fit is a dishonest metric and held-out predictive ability is the honest one. (Claim C.)
3. **Q:** On a trait controlled by many small effects spread across the genome, which of ridge, LASSO, and elastic net do you expect to predict best, and why?
   *Answer sketch:* Ridge-like behavior (ridge, or elastic net tilted toward L2) tends to win. LASSO aggressively zeros most coefficients, which is the wrong move when the truth is many small non-zero effects in LD — it discards real signal and arbitrarily picks one marker among correlated ones. Elastic net blends the two; with mostly-small-effect architecture it should behave ridge-like. (Claim A; the elastic-net-blend claim.)
4. **Q:** Name a concrete condition under which a tree ensemble would genuinely pull ahead of GBLUP, and the mechanism.
   *Answer sketch:* When real non-additive effects matter — strong epistasis (marker × marker interactions) or genotype-by-environment interaction — where the signal is genuinely non-linear, trees can model the interaction structure a purely additive linear model cannot represent. Also helps with larger n (more data to estimate the extra structure reliably). (Claim B.)

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** Describe a leakage-free cross-validation harness that compares GBLUP, elastic net, random forest, and gradient boosting fairly. What must be true about the folds and the hyperparameter tuning?
   *Answer sketch:* (1) Define the K folds once and run *every* method through the *same* folds (apples-to-apples). (2) In each fold, the held-out lines must be invisible to training — never used to fit, scale, or tune. (3) Tune each method's hyperparameters *inside* the training portion only (nested CV), so the test fold never leaks into model selection. (4) Compute held-out predicted-vs-observed correlation per fold, report mean ± SD. (Claim C; the nested-CV point prevents an inflated number.)
2. **Q:** You run the harness and get: GBLUP 0.50, elastic net 0.49, random forest 0.45, gradient boosting 0.47 (mean held-out correlation), each ± ~0.04. What do you conclude, and what would you *not* claim?
   *Answer sketch:* Conclude that the penalized linear model and GBLUP are competitive with or slightly ahead of the tree ensembles, consistent with additive architecture (Claim A) and with Azodi et al. (2019). Given the ~0.04 spread, the GBLUP/elastic-net gap over the trees is within fold-to-fold noise — so do *not* claim a method is "significantly better" without checking the differences against that spread. The ensembles' lower mean despite their flexibility is the expected wasted-capacity result.
3. **Q:** A colleague reports "92% accuracy" for their gradient-boosting genomic predictor and is thrilled. What single question do you ask first, and why?
   *Answer sketch:* "Is that training fit or held-out cross-validated predictive ability — and how were the folds made?" A 0.92 number is implausible as held-out predictive ability for a complex trait (capped by h² and relatedness) and screams either training fit (memorization) or leakage. The question separates a real result from an inflated one. (Claim C.)
4. **Q:** Using scikit-learn, sketch the calls to fit a cross-validated elastic net and a gradient-booster on a genotype matrix `X` and phenotype `y`, scored by held-out correlation. Concepts over exact syntax.
   *Answer sketch:* Use `KFold` to define folds; for elastic net use `ElasticNetCV` (it tunes `alpha`/`l1_ratio` by inner CV) or wrap `ElasticNet` in `GridSearchCV` inside each training fold; for boosting use `GradientBoostingRegressor` (or XGBoost) with hyperparameters tuned inside each training fold. In each fold: fit on train, predict on held-out, `np.corrcoef`/`pearsonr` of predicted vs observed; average across folds. Grade the *mechanics* (same folds, inner tuning, held-out correlation), not exact arguments.

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** Azodi et al. (2019) benchmarked twelve algorithms across 18 traits and six species. Reconstruct their headline finding and the two factors that moved the ranking.
   *Answer sketch:* Headline: across most traits, linear/parametric methods (rrBLUP, Bayesian models) were competitive with or better than the machine-learning methods — flexibility did not systematically help. The ranking depended on (i) genetic architecture (more additive → linear wins; non-additive → ML can gain) and (ii) training-population size (more data helps the flexible methods). They also found feature selection was critical for neural nets when markers vastly outnumbered training lines. (Claims A, B, C.)
2. **Q:** "More flexible models make better predictions." Take this claim apart in the genomic-prediction setting — when is it true, when false, and why?
   *Answer sketch:* It is true only when the added flexibility matches real structure in the data (non-additive effects, G×E) *and* there is enough data to estimate that structure reliably. It is false when the signal is additive and high-dimensional and n is limited: the extra flexibility fits noise, raising training fit but lowering held-out accuracy. So flexibility is not free — it must be earned by signal that the simpler model cannot represent. (Claims A, B.)
3. **Q:** A gradient booster shows the *largest* train-to-held-out drop of all methods on this dataset. Why is that the expected result, and what is the booster's flexibility "buying" on this trait?
   *Answer sketch:* Boosting is the most flexible contender, so on a small, additive dataset it most readily fits sample-specific noise — high training correlation, much lower held-out correlation, hence the biggest gap. On a mostly-additive trait its extra capacity buys almost no real signal (a linear sum already captures the additive effects), so the flexibility mostly translates into overfitting rather than prediction. (Claim C, Claim A.)
4. **Q:** Why might LASSO specifically underperform ridge on genomic data, beyond "it zeros coefficients"? Bring in linkage disequilibrium.
   *Answer sketch:* Markers are correlated (in LD), often in blocks. LASSO tends to pick one marker from a correlated group and zero the rest, which is unstable (small data changes flip which marker survives) and discards the shared signal carried redundantly across the block. With many small effects spread across LD blocks, ridge's "keep all, shrink all" both stabilizes and pools the redundant signal, predicting better. (Claim A; the elastic-net-blend claim.)

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** Your program must choose a default genomic-prediction method to deploy across dozens of traits, most additive but a few suspected to have G×E or epistasis. Make the call and justify it, including how you would detect the exceptions.
   *Answer sketch:* Default to a penalized linear / GBLUP model — it is competitive-or-better on additive traits, cheap, stable, and interpretable (Claim A). For the suspected non-additive/G×E traits, run the ensemble (or a G×E-aware model) *through the same leakage-free CV* and adopt it *only if* it beats the linear baseline by more than the fold-to-fold noise (Claim B, Claim C). The judgment: don't pay the flexibility cost everywhere; spend it only where held-out evidence shows non-additive signal to capture.
2. **Q:** Design an experiment that would *demonstrate* the claim "ML earns its keep only where non-additive effects matter," using simulation where you control the architecture.
   *Answer sketch:* Simulate phenotypes from the same markers under two regimes: (a) purely additive (effects sum), (b) strongly epistatic (marker × marker interaction terms). Hold n and marker count fixed. Run GBLUP/elastic net and gradient boosting/MLP through identical K-fold CV in both regimes; measure held-out predictive ability. Confirming result: under (a) the linear methods match or beat the ensembles; under (b) the ensembles pull ahead — isolating non-additivity as the cause (Claim B). Optionally sweep n to show the ensembles' advantage grows with sample size. Everything judged on held-out correlation (Claim C).

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: a more powerful / flexible model should predict better.** → **surfacing question:** "On a mostly-additive trait with ~600 lines, gradient boosting fits training best but loses to ridge out-of-sample. Why isn't its extra power helping?" (Correct: additive signal a linear model represents exactly; extra flexibility fits noise, not signal — flexibility must be earned by real non-additive structure. Claims A, B.)
- **Trap: higher training R²/accuracy means a better predictor.** → **surfacing question:** "Two models: one with 0.95 training correlation and 0.40 held-out; one with 0.55 training and 0.50 held-out. Which do you ship, and what does the first model's gap tell you?" (Correct: ship the second; the first overfit/memorized — training fit is not generalization. Claim C.)
- **Trap: LASSO is the right tool because it 'finds the important markers.'** → **surfacing question:** "For a many-small-effects trait with markers in LD blocks, what does LASSO do to a block of correlated markers, and why does that hurt prediction versus ridge?" (Correct: it keeps one and zeros the rest unstably, discarding pooled signal; ridge keeps-and-shrinks all and predicts better. Claim A.)
- **Trap: tree ensembles capture epistasis 'for free,' so they should beat linear models on real biology.** → **surfacing question:** "If a trait truly has strong epistasis but you have only a few hundred lines, will the ensemble reliably beat GBLUP? What does it need that you may not have?" (Correct: it needs enough data to estimate the interaction structure reliably; with small n even real epistasis may not be learnable, so the ensemble can still lose. Claim B with the sample-size caveat.)
- **Trap: cross-validation is just a formality; pick the model by which fits the data best.** → **surfacing question:** "If you tune hyperparameters on the full dataset and then report CV accuracy, what specifically have you leaked, and which direction does it bias the number?" (Correct: the test folds leaked into tuning, inflating the reported accuracy; tuning must live inside the training portion. Claim C.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** A chickpea program runs genomic prediction for two traits: (1) seed weight, a classic highly polygenic, largely additive trait, and (2) yield under terminal drought, where line performance depends heavily on the interaction between genotype and the specific drought timing of each environment (strong G×E). The team's ML enthusiast wants to deploy a tuned XGBoost model for *both* traits because "boosting wins every Kaggle competition." A second member insists on GBLUP for both. You have ~800 genotyped lines phenotyped across several drought environments. Answer: (1) For seed weight, predict which approach wins held-out predictive ability and explain the mechanism in terms of genetic architecture and wasted model capacity. (2) For drought yield, explain why the ranking could flip, naming the precise property of the trait that gives the flexible model something real to capture. (3) Specify the single experiment that would settle the dispute for *each* trait without fooling yourself, including how you handle hyperparameter tuning and what you would refuse to conclude from training fit. (4) State the one metric on which both methods must be compared and why a glowing training-fit number from XGBoost would be evidence of a problem, not of success.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) For seed weight, the penalized linear / GBLUP model should win or tie. The architecture is additive and high-dimensional; a linear model represents the sum of small additive effects exactly, while XGBoost spends its capacity hunting interactions that are not there and, with only ~800 lines, fits sample noise — wasted (and harmful) flexibility (Claim A). (2) For drought yield the ranking can flip because the trait has strong genotype-by-environment interaction — genuinely non-linear, interaction-driven structure that an additive linear model cannot represent but a tree ensemble (or a G×E-aware model) can; that is exactly the condition under which ML earns its keep (Claim B). (3) For each trait, run *both* methods through one shared, leakage-free K-fold cross-validation, tuning each method's hyperparameters *inside* each training fold only (nested CV) so the held-out fold never informs tuning; for drought yield the folds should reflect the real prediction scenario (e.g., hold out whole environments if predicting new environments). Adopt the flexible model only if it beats GBLUP by more than the fold-to-fold spread. Refuse to conclude anything from training fit (Claim C). (4) Compare both methods on cross-validated held-out predictive ability (predicted-vs-observed correlation in held-out folds). A glowing XGBoost *training-fit* number is evidence of memorization/overfitting, not skill, because any flexible model can drive training fit arbitrarily high; only the held-out number reflects ability to predict genuinely new lines (Claim C).
