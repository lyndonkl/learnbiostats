# p5m2 · Question Bank

Probing questions for the `biostat-assessor` to draw from, for [[p5m2-genotype-x-environment-interaction-and-stability|p5m2 · Genotype × Environment Interaction and Stability]]. Organized by Bloom level, ascending. The assessor never reveals answers first; it asks, waits, then probes the gap. "Answer sketches" below are for the assessor's grading only — they are not read to the learner.

One idea sits at the center of this module and every question should ladder back to it: **a line that wins in Oregon can lose in Florida because genotype-by-environment interaction (G×E) means the ranking of genotypes changes across environments.** "Best" is therefore not a fixed property of a genotype — it depends on where the genotype is grown. The operational consequence: **modelling G×E (a marker's shared main effect across environments *plus* its environment-specific effect) is what makes a genomic prediction deployable across the target population of environments**, and deployability must be judged by whether the model ranks genotypes correctly across that set of fields — not by a single pooled accuracy. Readings that ground these answers: Burgueño, de los Campos, Weigel & Crossa (2012), *Crop Science* 52:707–719 (multi-environment GBLUP modelling G×E via genetic correlations between environments); López-Cruz, Crossa et al. (2015), *G3* 5(4):569–582 (the marker × environment model that decomposes each marker effect into a shared main effect plus an environment-specific effect); Jarquín, Crossa et al. (2014), *Theoretical and Applied Genetics* 127:595–607 (reaction-norm model: marker × environmental-covariate interaction); and the stability classics Finlay & Wilkinson (1963), *Australian Journal of Agricultural Research* 14:742–754 (regression on the environmental mean) and Yan et al. (2000), *Crop Science* 40:597–605 (GGE biplot / mega-environments). *(All citations verified; see manifest.)*

## remember (retrieval — can you recall the primitive?)
1. **Q:** Write the standard multi-environment model that separates a genotype's mean performance into its three additive pieces, and name each term.
   *Answer sketch:* `y_ij = μ + g_i + e_j + (ge)_ij + ε_ij`. `μ` = grand mean; `g_i` = genotype main effect (genotype `i`'s deviation averaged across environments); `e_j` = environment main effect (environment `j`'s deviation); `(ge)_ij` = the genotype-by-environment interaction (the part of genotype `i`'s performance in environment `j` not explained by the two main effects); `ε_ij` = residual. The interaction term is the one this module is about. Reject answers that stop at `g_i + e_j` (the additive-only model that *assumes G×E away*).
2. **Q:** Define genotype-by-environment interaction in one sentence, in terms of *rankings*.
   *Answer sketch:* G×E is present when the relative performance (ranking) of genotypes changes across environments — i.e. the difference between two genotypes is not the same in every environment. The sharp form is a *crossover*: the order flips. Reject "the genotype does better in a good environment" alone — uniform scaling without rank change is non-crossover G×E (or none); the load-bearing case is the rank change.
3. **Q:** In the marker × environment (M×E) decomposition, each marker effect is split into two parts. Name them.
   *Answer sketch:* A **main (shared) effect** common to all environments, plus an **environment-specific effect** (a deviation for that marker in that particular environment). Capturing both is what lets the model represent ranking changes a pooled, shared-only model averages away (López-Cruz, Crossa et al. 2015).

## understand (explain in your own words)
1. **Q:** Why does "the best line" stop being a property of the genotype once G×E is present? Use the Oregon/Florida framing.
   *Answer sketch:* With G×E the ranking changes across environments, so a line that tops the trial in Oregon can sit mid-pack or lose in Florida. "Best" becomes a function of *where* — a (genotype, environment) pair — not a label attached to the genotype alone. A single mean across environments hides this: it can rank a flip-prone line above a line that quietly wins in the target region. The whole point of modelling G×E is to recover this where-dependence.
2. **Q:** Two genotypes have the *same* mean across all environments. What is *stability* measuring that the mean cannot tell you, and when would a breeder deliberately prefer the *less* stable one?
   *Answer sketch:* Stability measures how much a genotype's performance swings as the environment changes — the spread/slope of its reaction norm, not its average. Two lines can share a mean while one is flat (stable, dependable across the region) and the other swings high in good environments and low in poor ones (responsive/unstable). A breeder targeting *favorable, high-input* environments may deliberately want the responsive line (it captures the upside where it will be grown); a breeder targeting *marginal, variable* environments wants the stable one (it avoids catastrophic lows). The mean alone can't make that call.
3. **Q:** If you pool all environments and fit a single shared set of effects, where does the G×E "go"? Explain mechanistically what happens to a line whose ranking flips.
   *Answer sketch:* Ignoring the interaction term doesn't make G×E vanish — it gets dumped into the residual `ε` (inflating error) and into a *biased main effect*: the genotype gets one averaged value that is wrong in every environment it actually flips in. For a flip-prone line, the pooled model predicts something near its cross-environment average, which describes no real field — it over-predicts where the line dips and under-predicts where it spikes. That is why a pooled prediction can be useless precisely for the genotypes G×E matters most for.
4. **Q:** Burgueño et al. (2012) model G×E through *genetic correlations between environments*. Explain in your own words why that lets you "borrow information" and beat a pooled model.
   *Answer sketch:* The model treats a genotype's value in each environment as correlated-but-distinct traits; the estimated genetic correlation between two environments says how much performance in one informs performance in the other. When environments are highly correlated, the model borrows strength across them (more effective data per genotype, better estimates); when they are weakly or negatively correlated (strong G×E), it keeps their predictions appropriately separate instead of forcing a single shared value. A pooled model implicitly assumes correlation = 1 everywhere, which is wrong under G×E and is exactly what degrades it.

## apply (use it on a new, concrete case)
1. **Q:** Here is a tiny MET (mean yield). Env1: A=8, B=6, C=4. Env2: A=4, B=6, C=8. Compute each genotype's mean, identify the G×E, and say what a pooled, mean-based recommendation would get wrong.
   *Answer sketch:* Means: A=(8+4)/2=6, B=6, C=6 — *identical*. Yet the rankings fully reverse (A best in Env1, worst in Env2; C the mirror; B flat). This is a textbook crossover G×E with zero main-effect difference. A pooled mean-based recommendation calls all three "equal" and is blind to the fact that A is the right choice for Env1-like fields and C for Env2-like fields. The information lives entirely in the interaction term, which the mean discards.
2. **Q:** Using the same data, sketch the Finlay–Wilkinson picture: regress each genotype on the environmental mean and interpret the slopes. (Environmental means: Env1 = 6, Env2 = 6.)
   *Answer sketch:* Careful — here both environment means equal 6, so the environmental-mean axis is degenerate and the classic F–W regression can't separate the genotypes (it needs environments that differ in mean). The teaching point: F–W works by ordering environments along their *mean productivity* and reading each genotype's slope (slope ≈ 1 = average responsiveness, >1 = responsive to good environments, <1 = stable/buffered). A strong answer notices the degeneracy and proposes data where environment means differ, then states what each slope would mean. (Accept: "you can't run F–W meaningfully when all environment means are equal; you'd need environments of differing productivity.")
3. **Q:** You have markers and yield for the same lines across 6 environments. Specify a shared-only model and an M×E (shared + environment-specific) model for these data, and state, before fitting, which genotypes the M×E model should help predict better.
   *Answer sketch:* Shared-only: one vector of marker effects, same in all environments (e.g. RR-BLUP/GBLUP on pooled data) — predicts each line's cross-environment value. M×E (López-Cruz et al. 2015): each marker effect = shared main effect + environment-specific deviation, so the model can produce different rankings per environment. It should help most for the genotypes whose lines *cross* in the interaction plot — the flip-prone, low-stability lines — and in environments weakly correlated with the rest; for genotypes that are flat/stable and for tightly-correlated environments, the two models converge.

## analyze (take it apart; compare; find the assumption)
1. **Q:** A pooled genomic model and an M×E model report nearly the *same* overall accuracy on a random hold-out, yet the M×E model is the deployable one. Reconcile this — how can equal accuracy hide a real difference, and what validation would expose it?
   *Answer sketch:* A random hold-out mixes (genotype, environment) cells from every environment, so a pooled model gets credit for the many cells where rankings happen to agree, masking its failure on the flip-prone cells — the averaged accuracy looks fine. The difference lives in *cross-environment* deployment: predicting genotypes in an environment the model was not trained on. **Leave-one-environment-out (LOEO)** cross-validation exposes it: train on some environments, predict an unseen one, and check per-environment whether the model recovers the ranking changes. The M×E / reaction-norm model can carry information into the new environment (via genetic correlations or environmental covariates); the pooled model can only repeat its average and will mis-rank wherever that environment's ranking differs.
2. **Q:** "Just average over environments and pick the top line." Take this apart: list the assumptions buried in the averaging strategy and identify which one G×E violates first.
   *Answer sketch:* Buried assumptions: (i) rankings are stable across environments (so the mean preserves order); (ii) the target population of environments is well-represented by the trial set and weighted as you'd deploy; (iii) the breeder cares only about mean, not stability/risk. G×E violates (i) first and hardest — under crossover interaction the mean *reverses* the order in some environments, so the top-mean line can be the wrong release for parts of the target region. Strong answers note (ii) too: an unrepresentative or unevenly-weighted environment sample biases even the mean.
3. **Q:** Compare what Burgueño et al. (2012) model (genetic correlations between environments) with what Jarquín et al. (2014) model (marker × environmental-covariate interaction). What can the reaction-norm/covariate approach do that the correlation approach cannot, and at what cost?
   *Answer sketch:* The 2012 correlation approach learns how *observed* environments relate and borrows strength among them — but it can only predict into environments it has data/correlations for; a brand-new environment with no trial data is out of reach (it's identified by an index, not described). The 2014 reaction-norm approach interacts markers with *environmental covariates* (weather, soil), so a genotype's value becomes a *function* of measurable environmental variables — letting the model predict into a *new* environment described only by its covariates, even with no yield data there. Cost: you must measure/obtain reliable environmental covariates and assume the covariate→performance function generalizes; it adds dimensionality and modelling assumptions. The choice depends on the deployment goal: rank known environments better (correlation) vs reach genuinely new ones (covariates).

## evaluate / create (judge a tradeoff; design something)
1. **Q:** A breeder must recommend, for next season, a single model to choose varieties for a *target population of environments* spanning a humid coastal zone and a dry inland zone whose trial rankings are known to disagree. Judge between (a) a shared-only pooled model, (b) an M×E model, and (c) carving the region into two mega-environments (GGE-biplot style) and modelling each separately. State the conditions under which each is the right call.
   *Answer sketch:* (a) Shared-only is right only if G×E across the two zones is small/non-crossover — but the premise says rankings disagree, so it will mis-rank in at least one zone; reject for this case. (b) M×E is the general workhorse when there is meaningful but not total disagreement: it captures shared signal *and* zone-specific deviations, borrowing strength while allowing rank changes — deployable across the whole TPE. (c) Mega-environment splitting (Yan et al. 2000) is right when the two zones are so distinct and internally stable that they are effectively *separate breeding targets* — then fit/select within each and recommend zone-specific varieties. The deciding question: is the region one TPE with structured G×E (use M×E) or two TPEs masquerading as one (split into mega-environments)? A strong answer ties the choice to the magnitude/structure of the genetic correlation between zones and to whether the breeder will release one variety region-wide or different varieties per zone.
2. **Q:** Design a cross-validation experiment that *honestly* tests whether a G×E model is deployable across a target population of environments, and that would catch a model that merely memorizes environments it has seen. Specify the split, what you measure, and the failure mode you are guarding against.
   *Answer sketch:* Use **leave-one-environment-out** (and, if covariates exist, leave-one-*environment-type*-out): repeatedly hold out an entire environment, train on the rest, and predict the untested genotypes in the held-out environment. Measure **per-environment** predictive correlation/ranking accuracy (and specifically whether crossover rankings are recovered), not a single pooled R². Guard against: a model that looks accurate only because random hold-outs leak same-environment and related-genotype information (memorization), which collapses the moment it must predict into an environment it never saw — exactly the deployment situation. A strong design also reports where the simpler pooled model *wins* (tightly correlated environments) so the verdict is honest, and ties "deployable" back to ranking genotypes correctly across the TPE (core claim), not to average fit.

## Misconception traps
Common wrong mental models for this module, and the question that surfaces each:
- **Trap: the best line is the best line everywhere — performance ranking is a fixed property of the genotype.** → **surfacing question:** "A line wins your Oregon trial. Can it *lose* in Florida even with no measurement error? What is that telling you about the word 'best'?" (Correct: yes — G×E means the ranking changes across environments, so 'best' is a (genotype, environment) property, not a genotype label.)
- **Trap: G×E is just noise — a higher mean and a tighter design make it go away.** → **surfacing question:** "If you replicate until the standard errors are tiny and the rankings *still* reverse between two environments, is that noise? Where does that signal live in the model?" (Correct: no — it's a real, structured effect, the `(ge)_ij` interaction term; replication shrinks `ε`, not G×E.)
- **Trap: a genotype's mean across environments is enough to rank and recommend it.** → **surfacing question:** "Two lines have identical means across all environments. Are they equally good choices for a breeder, and what would distinguish them?" (Correct: no — they can differ entirely in stability/responsiveness; the mean hides the reaction norm and the ranking flips.)
- **Trap: pooling all environments into one model just gives a robust 'average' prediction and loses nothing important.** → **surfacing question:** "When you pool and fit shared-only effects, where does the interaction go — and what does the pooled prediction look like for a line whose ranking flips?" (Correct: G×E goes into inflated residual and a biased main effect; the pooled value describes no real field and is worst exactly for flip-prone lines.)
- **Trap: a single high pooled accuracy proves the model is deployable across the region.** → **surfacing question:** "Your model has high accuracy on a random hold-out. Why might it fail when you ask it to rank genotypes in an environment it never trained on, and what validation would catch that?" (Correct: random hold-out leaks environment/relatedness; deployability needs leave-one-environment-out, judged per environment on ranking recovery.)
- **Trap: modelling G×E means fitting a totally separate model per environment.** → **surfacing question:** "Why is splitting into one independent model per environment usually *worse* than an M×E model, in terms of the data each genotype effectively gets?" (Correct: independent per-environment models throw away the shared main effect and the correlation between environments — they don't borrow strength; M×E keeps shared signal *and* environment-specific deviations.)

## Transfer prompt
One question that forces the concept into an unfamiliar crop-breeding scenario.
- **Q:** The unfamiliar case is a dry bean (common bean) breeding program serving smallholder farmers across a target population of environments that splits into two contrasting production systems: high-rainfall highland plots with cool nights, and low-rainfall lowland plots that routinely hit terminal drought and heat. The program ran a multi-environment trial of the same 200 RILs across 8 sites (4 highland, 4 lowland) with genotyping. An analyst pooled all 8 environments, fit one shared set of marker effects, reported a strong random-hold-out accuracy, and recommended releasing the single top-ranked line region-wide. A field officer pushes back: the top line came from a highland site and "doesn't do anything" in the lowland fields. (1) Diagnose, using the multi-environment model and the idea of crossover G×E, what the pooled analysis got wrong and where the lowland information went. (2) The program can collect environmental covariates (rainfall, max temperature, soil water-holding capacity) at every site. Propose two concretely different modelling routes that would make the prediction deployable across this TPE — one that borrows information via correlations among the *observed* sites, and one that could predict into a *new* lowland site described only by its covariates — and name the specific published model family behind each. (3) Specify the cross-validation scheme that would have caught the pooled model's failure before release, and say what you would measure. (4) Finally, decide whether the program should release one variety region-wide or pursue mega-environment-specific releases, and tie that decision to the genetic correlation between highland and lowland.
   *Answer sketch:* (1) The pooled shared-only model assumed a single ranking across all 8 sites (implicitly genetic correlation = 1 between highland and lowland). With crossover G×E between the cool-highland and drought-lowland systems, the highland-favoring top line's superiority does not transfer; the lowland signal didn't vanish — it was dumped into inflated residual and a biased main effect, so the pooled "top line" is an average that describes neither system well and is wrong for the lowland fields. The strong random-hold-out accuracy was inflated by leaking same-site and related-RIL information and is irrelevant to ranking lines in a system the model must generalize to. (2) Route A — model G×E via *genetic correlations between environments* across the observed 8 sites (Burgueño, de los Campos, Weigel & Crossa 2012, multi-environment GBLUP), or equivalently an M×E decomposition giving each marker a shared main effect plus a highland- and lowland-specific effect (López-Cruz, Crossa et al. 2015); this borrows strength among correlated sites while letting rankings differ between systems. Route B — a *reaction-norm* model interacting markers with the environmental covariates (rainfall, temperature, soil water) (Jarquín, Crossa et al. 2014), so a line's predicted yield is a function of the covariates and the model can predict into a brand-new lowland site described only by its weather/soil, with no yield data there. (3) Use **leave-one-environment-out** (better: leave-one-*system*-out — hold out all 4 lowland sites, train on highland + remaining, predict lowland RILs), measuring per-environment ranking/predictive accuracy and specifically whether crossover rankings are recovered — not a single pooled R². That split reproduces the actual deployment ("predict lines in a system we haven't phenotyped them in") and would have shown the pooled model collapsing in the lowland. (4) The release decision turns on the estimated genetic correlation between highland and lowland: if it is high (rankings largely agree), one region-wide variety chosen by an M×E/correlation model is defensible; if it is low or negative (the crossover the field officer reports), treat highland and lowland as two **mega-environments** (Yan et al. 2000) and pursue system-specific releases, selecting within each. This is the core claim transferred intact: because rankings change across environments, deployability means modelling shared *and* environment-specific effects and judging the model by correct ranking across the target population of environments — not by a pooled accuracy that averages the disagreement away.
